## Supporting evidence

1. Papp, L. M., Kouros, C. D., & Cummings, E. M. (2009). Demand-Withdraw Patterns in Marital Conflict in the Home. Personal Relationships 16(2), 285–300. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3218801/ ; DOI 10.1111/j.1475-6811.2009.01223.x.
   - Diary reports from 116 US married couples. Defines demand-withdraw as one partner attempting to discuss a problem while the other avoids or ends discussion; links the pattern with negative emotions/tactics and less conflict resolution.
   - Observational association, not proof that a particular partner's action caused a particular outcome. Married US sample does not establish universal effects across cultures or every relationship. Roles are interactional, not a gender or attachment diagnosis.
2. Brittini Carter, LMHC, The Gottman Institute. Self-Soothing Tips for High Conflict Couples. Published May 16, 2022; updated May 17, 2026. https://www.gottman.com/blog/self-soothing-tips-for-high-conflict-couples/
   - Practitioner guidance: identify escalating arousal, take a break, self-soothe, and return when both can participate constructively.
   - Educational advice, not a clinical trial testing this episode's exact wording. No guaranteed duration or guaranteed success asserted.
