## Primary Empirical Sources

### 1. Luxembourg National Fare-Free Public Transport — 6-Year Evaluation (LISER, 2026)
- **Citation:** Van Acker, V. (September 2026). *Fare-free public transport: clear social gains, limited congestion relief.* LISER Policy Brief, Issue 2026-17. Luxembourg Institute of Socio-Economic Research & Ghent University.
- **URL:** https://liser.lu/assets/Fare-free-public-transport-clear-social-gain-limited-congestion-relief-2026-17-en.pdf
- **Documented findings:**
  - Luxembourg introduced full nationwide FFPT in March 2020 across bus, tram, and standard-class rail, funded by general taxation. Prior to reform, ticket revenues covered ~8% of the €500M annual operating budget (~€41M; ~6% bus, ~10% rail).
  - **Limited car substitution on its own:** Comparing 2020 vs. autumn 2023 weighted panel surveys, daily car use fell only **4.6 percentage points** (34.5% → 29.9%), while daily bus use rose 11.2% → 12.7% and daily train use 7.7% → 10.5%. Among 2020 "car-only" users, only **1.2%** switched exclusively to public transport; far more became **multimodal** (14.7% Car + PT; 17.5% Car + PT + Active Transport; overall multimodal share rose from 77.8% → 86.2%).
  - **Clear social & participation gains:** Among riders whose PT use changed, **47.9%** used PT more often for work/education, **47.3%** for cultural activities, and **39.5%** for tourism/leisure—concentrated among young adults (16–34), students, unemployed people, households with children, and below-median income earners. On weekdays 56% (weekends 54%) said reintroducing fares would moderately or significantly hurt their lives.
  - **Active mobility & quality warning signs:** Walking actually *increased* alongside PT (daily walking 36.5% → 40.8%; only 10.4% replaced walking with PT), while daily cycling dipped slightly (3.7% → 3.2%). Overall service satisfaction stayed slightly positive, **but comfort, cleanliness, and perceived safety declined**, underscoring that capacity and service quality require sustained investment.
- **Qualifications:** Survey period overlaps with COVID-19, teleworking, fuel-price inflation, and tram expansion; pre-reform fares were already low (€2/2h, €4/day), so direct price shock was smaller than in high-fare cities.

### 2. European Case-Study Synthesis: Tallinn, Dunkirk, Hasselt & Luxembourg (EU Urban Mobility Observatory, 2021)
- **Citation:** European Commission — EU Urban Mobility Observatory (28 April 2021). *Free passenger transport – exploring the benefits and disadvantages.*
- **URL:** https://urban-mobility-observatory.transport.ec.europa.eu/resources/case-studies/free-passenger-transport-exploring-benefits-and-disadvantages_en
- **Documented findings:**
  - **Tallinn (Estonia, since 2013, pop. ~420,000):** Ticket sales previously covered ~1/3 of operating costs (€12M in 2012). Early evaluations found PT share rose ~3% overall (~1.2% directly attributed to FFPT; Cats et al., 2017 later reported ~14% PT ridership increase), car use fell ~5%, **but average trip length dropped 10%**, indicating some modal shift from walking to short bus trips. Among low-income, out-of-education, and unemployed groups, PT modal share jumped **+23%**. Revenue loss was offset because ~25,000 residents registered in the city (municipal income-tax allocation). Still, more than half of work trips remained by car.
  - **Dunkirk (France, since 2018, pop. ~200,000):** Paired universal free buses with a major **network overhaul and fleet expansion** (100 → 135 buses, +7 new lines, 3 exchange hubs). Initial VIGS evaluation (Jan–May 2019 vs. 2017) recorded **+85% ridership** (+65% weekdays, **+120% weekends**). Half of users were new; 48% of new users reported shifting from cars (24% overall passenger shift from car), though aggregate citywide car mode share fell only modestly (~3%). Funded via an increased employer mobility payroll tax (*versement mobilité*, 70% of funding) plus municipal budget (30%); prior farebox had been only ~10% of operating costs.
  - **Hasselt (Belgium, 1997–2014, pop. ~75,000):** Free bus network expanded from 8 → 46 buses (4 → 9 routes) and ridership rose 12×, **but operating costs quadrupled in 10 years** (€967k in 1997 → €3.4M in 2007). After the 2008 financial crisis, municipal budgets could no longer cover the cost and fares were reintroduced in 2014 (keeping free travel for youth <19 and seniors).
- **Qualifications:** Dunkirk's ridership jump combined fare abolition with major fleet and route expansion; Hasselt shows fiscal vulnerability when operating costs grow faster than municipal revenue.

### 3. Bus Dwell Time, All-Door Boarding & Fare Collection Bottlenecks (NACTO, 2017)
- **Citation:** National Association of City Transportation Officials (February 2017). *Better Boarding, Better Buses: Streamlining Boarding & Fares.* Practitioners' Paper #1.
- **URL:** https://nacto.org/wp-content/uploads/NACTO_Better-Buses_Boarding.pdf
- **Documented findings:**
  - **Dwell-time bottleneck:** Stopping to load/unload passengers (*dwell time*) can take **up to one-third of total bus travel time**. Front-door cash/card payment takes **nearly 5 seconds (and up to 9 seconds) per passenger**, making conventional front-door buses *congestible*—the more riders board, the slower the bus gets.
  - **All-door boarding physical effect:** Eliminating driver fare control and letting riders board through all doors cuts per-passenger dwell time sharply: **San Francisco Muni** cut dwell times **38% per passenger** (37% overall, 42% more consistent); **NYC Select Bus Service** cut total stop time **28–40%** and per-passenger dwell time **42–51%**; **Chicago Belmont pilot** saw ~**50%** dwell-time reduction.
  - **Cash/payment overhead:** Eliminating on-board cash saved Transport for London nearly £26M/year; U.S. agencies lose an estimated 6M bus-hours/year ($700M agency cost) at stops due to payment delays.
- **Qualifications:** Most NACTO case studies used off-board proof-of-payment (smartcard/ticket machines + inspectors) rather than zero fares; fare-free transit removes the remaining validation and inspection layer completely, though heavy crowding still slows boarding if bus capacity is not expanded.

### 4. Farebox Recovery, Crowding Friction & Opportunity-Cost Trade-offs (Vital City / NYC Pilot Analysis, 2024)
- **Citation:** Celestin, A. (2 October 2024). *Free Fares, for Better and Worse.* Vital City (citing NYC MTA 5-route fare-free bus pilot 2023–2024, Kansas City pilot, and IBO estimates).
- **URL:** https://www.vitalcitynyc.org/should-new-york-city-have-free-fares-for-better-and-worse/
- **Documented findings:**
  - **Pilot ridership vs. crowding dwell slowdown:** NYC's year-long 5-route free bus pilot boosted ridership **+30% weekdays and +38% weekends**, and **assaults on bus drivers dropped nearly 40%** (consistent with Kansas City) because fare disputes disappeared. However, only **~11%** of new riders came from cars/taxis, and without extra buses, **free buses spent 7% more time at bus stops** than system average due to crowding.
  - **Ticketing & enforcement overhead saved:** Fare systems carry major capital and operating costs (e.g., ~$700k per station for anti-evasion fare gates, ~$35M/year in private fare-gate security, card-reader contracts).
  - **Budget opportunity cost:** Replacing high farebox recovery (e.g., MTA ~26% operating budget, ~$5.2B/yr full system; ~$652M–$778M/yr buses alone) competes directly with capital/service investments (new buses, frequency, reliability, accessibility). Surveys consistently show prospective riders prioritize **frequency, reliability, and reach** over fare price alone.
- **Qualifications:** Commentary/policy synthesis grounded in NYC MTA and IBO figures; illustrates high-farebox-recovery megacity dynamics vs. low-farebox-recovery smaller European cities.
