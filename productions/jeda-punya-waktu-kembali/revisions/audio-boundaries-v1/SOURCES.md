## Evidence

1. [The Family Institute at Northwestern University — Trouble Communicating with your Partner? Top Five Tips from a Couples Therapist](https://www.family-institute.org/behavioral-health-resources/trouble-communicating-your-partner-top-five-tips-couples-therapist). Primary institutional practice guidance, especially section 4. It recommends naming one's own need for a timeout, specifying a time/place to resume, partner agreement, calming activities rather than preparing attacks, and actually returning. A repeat timeout may be needed; perfect resolution is not the immediate goal. This is a therapist's advisory application of established approaches, not a randomized trial of this episode's dialogue.
2. [The Gottman Institute — The Four Horsemen: Stonewalling](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-four-horsemen-stonewalling/). Primary practice guidance. Agree beforehand on a recognizable, respectful pause signal; explain feeling overwhelmed; take the interval to self-soothe. A unilateral departure may be interpreted negatively. Its broader diagnostic labels, gender discussion, numerical predictions and universal-sounding outcome language are not repeated in this episode.
3. [The Gottman Institute — How to Practice Self Soothing](https://www.gottman.com/blog/how-to-practice-self-soothing/). Primary practice guidance supporting a shared timeout signal and calming activities during the break. Used for the calming interval, not a guaranteed physiological recovery deadline.
4. [Papp, Kouros & Cummings (2009), Demand-Withdraw Patterns in Marital Conflict in the Home](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3218801/). Original observational diary study: 116 U.S. married couples recorded conflict over 15 days. Demand–withdraw patterns were associated with more negative interaction and lower conflict resolution. Not causal proof, not evidence that any single pause equals withdrawal or predicts a relationship's future. Background viability only; no study effect size or named pattern is asserted in the narration.
