## Research Questions Resolved

1. **Does mandatory pay transparency actually reduce pay gaps and unfair pay disparities?**
   **Yes — consistently across private-sector and public-sector studies**, shrinking gender pay gaps by ~13–30% (and up to 50% in some institutions) and cutting overall within-unit pay dispersion by up to 20%.
2. **How do ordinary employees react immediately when coworkers' salaries become visible?**
   **Asymmetrically.** Workers who discover they earn *below* peers in the same role suffer sharp drops in job satisfaction, reduce work effort, and actively search for new jobs — while workers paid *above* peers show little to no satisfaction gain. Across an entire society (Norway), online income visibility widened the rich–poor happiness gap by 29%.
3. **Why don't salaries simply rise to match the highest-paid coworker? What is the hidden mechanism?**
   **Bargaining-power flip (wage compression) and pay-for-performance decoupling.** When pay is secret, an employer can grant one standout worker a quiet raise without triggering demands from everyone else. When pay is public, raising one worker forces the employer to raise every peer too — so firms credibly commit to a *lower, flatter maximum wage*. Empirically, internal pay transparency reduces average wages by ~1–4% (and up to ~7% for top municipal managers, whose quit rate jumped 75%) and significantly weakens the slope linking individual pay to measurable performance.
4. **What condition flips the outcome from wage compression to higher worker pay?**
   **Cross-firm transparency and job mobility.** Economic theory and recent US job-posting salary-range data distinguish *within-firm* disclosure (which strengthens employer commitment to flat/lower pay, especially where workers bargain individually) from *cross-firm* transparency (which helps workers compare outside offers and switch to higher-paying employers, pushing wages up ~1.3–3.6% in recent US working-paper evidence).

## Primary Empirical Sources

### 1. Equilibrium Wage Compression & Employer Bargaining Power
- **Citation:** Zoë B. Cullen & Bobak Pakzad-Hurson (2023), *"Equilibrium Effects of Pay Transparency"*, *Econometrica* 91(3): 765–802.
- **URLs:**
  - Authors' summary (Microeconomic Insights, 2024): https://microeconomicinsights.org/equilibrium-effects-of-pay-transparency/
  - Journal record (IDEAS/RePEc): https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/emetrp/v91y2023i3p765-802.html
- **Method & scope:** Dynamic bargaining model + event-study difference-in-differences across 13 US states that enacted "Right of Workers to Talk" (ROWTT) pay-transparency laws (2004–2016), plus cross-country meta-analysis.
- **Documented findings:**
  - Internal pay transparency lowers pay inequality **and lowers average wages**.
  - **Core mechanism:** Without transparency, firms quietly accept high-performer raise demands rather than lose them. With transparency, firms credibly say *"if I give you a raise, I have to raise everyone else too — I can't afford it."* Anticipating horizontal renegotiation spillovers, employers commit to a lower maximum posted wage, transferring bargaining power from individual workers to the employer.
  - Across 13 US states, ROWTT laws reduced full-time average wages by **~2%** over 3 years.
  - Effect was **negligible in unionized occupations** (low individual bargaining), but **−4%** on average among college-educated workers with high individual bargaining power.
- **Caveats / crucial qualification:** Applies to *internal* (within-firm) transparency. The authors explicitly caution that *cross-firm* pay transparency (revealing competitor salaries) can have opposite equilibrium effects — intensifying inter-firm wage competition and empowering workers to move to better-paying employers.

### 2. Asymmetric Morale & Quit Intentions Among Below-Median Earners
- **Citation:** David Card, Alexandre Mas, Enrico Moretti & Emmanuel Saez (2012), *"Inequality at Work: The Effect of Peer Salaries on Job Satisfaction"*, *American Economic Review* 102(6): 2981–3003 (NBER Working Paper 16396).
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w16396
- **Method & scope:** Randomized field experiment informing a subset of University of California employees about a newly launched public website listing all UC employee salaries, followed by surveys on job satisfaction and job-search intentions.
- **Documented findings:**
  - Website usage doubled in the treated group; the vast majority looked up **colleagues in their own department**.
  - **Asymmetric reaction:** Workers earning *below* the median for their pay unit and occupation reported **significantly lower pay and job satisfaction** and a **significant increase in likelihood of looking for a new job**.
  - Workers earning *above* the median reported **no increase in satisfaction** and no change in job-search intentions.
- **Caveats:** Public-sector university setting with structured pay grades; measures short-run stated job satisfaction and search intention rather than multi-year realized turnover across private firms.

### 3. Peer vs. Manager Salary Comparisons in Southeast Asia
- **Citation:** Zoë Cullen & Ricardo Perez-Truglia (2022), *"How Much Does Your Boss Make? The Effects of Salary Comparisons"*, *Journal of Political Economy* 130(3): 766–822 (NBER Working Paper 24841; also summarized in Cullen 2023 HBS Working Paper 23-039 *"Is Pay Transparency Good?"*).
- **URLs:**
  - Journal page: https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/717891
  - HBS synthesis by author: https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/23-039_f20d86a0-a1cf-4bd6-8066-74ccc6f2c3cf.pdf
- **Method & scope:** Natural field experiment with **2,060 employees** at a large commercial bank in a lower-middle-income country in **Southeast Asia**, linking incentivized beliefs to administrative behavioral records (hours worked, emails sent, sales revenue).
- **Documented findings:**
  - Employees systematically underestimated manager salaries (−14.1% on average) and misperceived peer pay.
  - **Horizontal (peer) comparison demoralizes:** A 10% upward shift in perceived *peer* salary reduced daily hours worked by **9.43%** (elasticity −0.943).
  - **Vertical (manager) comparison motivates (when promotion is attainable):** A 10% upward shift in perceived *manager* salary increased hours worked by **1.5%**, emails sent by **1.3%**, and sales revenue by **1.1%** (career-concerns / promotion aspiration channel).
- **Caveats:** Single large corporate bank (highly relevant to Southeast Asian white-collar context, though behavioral elasticities come from targeted belief shocks rather than permanent society-wide public disclosure).

### 4. Narrowing Gender Pay Gaps via Slower Male Wage Growth (Denmark)
- **Citation:** Morten Bennedsen, Elena Simintzi, Margarita Tsoutsoura & Daniel Wolfenzon (2022), *"Do Firms Respond to Gender Pay Gap Transparency?"*, *The Journal of Finance* 77(4): 2051–2091 (NBER Working Paper 25435).
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w25435
- **Method & scope:** Difference-in-differences and difference-in-discontinuities analysis of Denmark's 2006 pay-transparency mandate requiring firms above 35 employees to report gender-disaggregated wage statistics using linked employer-employee administrative records.
- **Documented findings:**
  - Reduced the gender pay gap by **~2 percentage points** (**~13% reduction** relative to pre-law mean).
  - **How it narrowed:** Primarily by **slowing wage growth for male employees**, rather than sharply raising everyone's pay upward.
  - Firms also hired and internally promoted more women.
  - Despite a lower overall wage bill, firm profitability did not increase — consistent with an offsetting slight drop in labor productivity (~2.7% in log sales per employee, per Duchini et al. 2023 literature review).
- **Caveats:** Mandated aggregate gender-disaggregated statistics within firms (not named individual public slips), though the downward-compression mechanism matches individual disclosure studies.

### 5. Public "Sunshine List" Individual Disclosure (Canada)
- **Citation:** Michael Baker, Yosh Halberstam, Kory Kroft, Alexandre Mas & Derek Messacar (2023), *"Pay Transparency and the Gender Gap"*, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* 15(2): 157–183 (Statistics Canada Analytical Studies Branch Research Paper Series 11F0019M No. 430, 2019).
- **URL:** https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11f0019m/11f0019m2019018-eng.htm
- **Method & scope:** Event-study analysis of staggered Canadian provincial public-sector salary disclosure laws ("Sunshine Lists" naming individual faculty above CAD $50k–$100k) using administrative university faculty records since 1970.
- **Documented findings:**
  - Reduced average faculty salaries by **~1–3 percentage points**.
  - Reduced the gender pay gap by **~2.2–2.4 percentage points** (**~30% reduction** from a 7–8% baseline), primarily by slowing male salary growth, with smaller positive female gains (~1 pp) concentrated in unionized universities with formal grievance procedures.
- **Caveats:** Public-sector university faculty above disclosure thresholds; partial-equilibrium effect within higher education.

### 6. Pay Equity Up, Pay-for-Performance Down (20-Year US Academic Panel)
- **Citation:** Tomasz Obloj & Todd R. Zenger (2022), *"The influence of pay transparency on (gender) inequity, inequality and the performance basis of pay"*, *Nature Human Behaviour* 6: 646–655.
- **URL:** https://communities.springernature.com/posts/what-happens-to-pay-allocation-when-wages-becomes-transparent
- **Method & scope:** Longitudinal tracking of salaries and objective performance outputs (publications, grants, books) for nearly **100,000 US academics** across 8 US states over two decades before and after searchable public salary websites launched.
- **Documented findings:**
  - Substantially reduced **pay inequity** (attributable for up to **50%** of observed gender pay-gap narrowing in some institutions, via larger raises to unfairly underpaid staff).
  - Substantially reduced overall **pay inequality** (up to **20%** drop in within-department pay variance).
  - **Key trade-off:** Significantly **weakened the link between pay and measurable performance**, and shrank marginal financial returns to rank promotions — making pay flatter and less merit-differentiated.
- **Caveats:** US public universities with relatively visible academic output metrics; authors note that even where performance is objectively observable, transparency still flattens performance-linked pay.

### 7. Top-Earner Compression & Quit Surge (California Cities)
- **Citation:** Alexandre Mas (2017), *"Does Transparency Lead to Pay Compression?"*, *Journal of Political Economy* 125(5): 1683–1721 (NBER Working Paper 20558).
- **URLs:**
  - Journal abstract: https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/693137
  - NBER record: https://www.nber.org/papers/w20558
- **Method & scope:** Natural experiment exploiting a 2010 California mandate requiring municipal salaries to be posted online.
- **Documented findings:**
  - Among top city managers, public disclosure led to **~7% average compensation declines** (largely nominal) and a **75% increase in quit rates** relative to managers in cities that already disclosed pay.
  - Cuts were concentrated on higher initial pay levels due to public/social aversion to high visible pay rather than objective performance mispricing.
- **Caveats:** Public-sector executives subject to voter/taxpayer scrutiny; illustrates how visible top pay triggers public backlash, compression, and top-level exits.

### 8. Society-Wide Public Income Records & Social Well-Being (Norway *Skattelister*)
- **Citation:** Ricardo Perez-Truglia (2020), *"The Effects of Income Transparency on Well-Being: Evidence from a Natural Experiment"*, *American Economic Review* 110(4): 1019–1054.
- **URL:** https://www.aeaweb.org/research/income-transparency-happiness-well-being-norway
- **Method & scope:** Natural experiment using Norway's 2001 move putting centuries-old public tax records online (`Skattelister.no`), comparing high- vs. low-Internet-access Norwegians across 1985–2013 surveys and benchmarking against Germany (non-public records).
- **Documented findings:**
  - Nearly **a quarter of Norwegians** searched the tax site in 2013 — overwhelmingly looking up **non-famous peers (coworkers, friends, relatives)** rather than celebrities.
  - Putting incomes online increased the **happiness gap** between higher- and lower-income people by **29%** and the **life-satisfaction gap** by **21%** (lower-income people felt noticeably worse; richer people felt modestly prouder).
  - In **2014**, Norway reformed the system so searches were no longer anonymous (the person searched receives notification) to curb casual snooping while keeping transparency benefits.
- **Caveats:** Covers total taxable income across society, not only workplace base salaries; shows the real-world social-comparison cost of full public disclosure.

### 9. Cross-Firm Job-Posting Transparency Counterweight (Working Paper)
- **Citation:** David Arnold, Simon Quach & Bledi Taska (2025), *"The Impact of Pay Transparency in Job Postings on the Labor Market"*, NBER Working Paper 34480 (non-peer-reviewed working paper; complemented by Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson 2023 theoretical cross-firm distinction).
- **URLs:**
  - Authors' manuscript: https://darnold199.github.io/aq_manuscript.pdf
  - Contextual summary citing NBER w34480: https://peopleanalyst.com/magazine/the-pay-you-cant-explain
- **Documented findings:** State laws requiring salary ranges in external job postings increased posted wages by roughly **1.3–3.6%** across datasets by strengthening cross-employer competition, without measurable drops in employment.
- **Caveats:** Working paper (not yet peer-reviewed) and studies *external job-posting ranges* rather than named public slips of incumbent workers; supports our 100-world sensitivity insight that **cross-firm job mobility** is the decisive variable that can tip transparency from employer-favored compression toward worker-favored competition.

## Claim-to-Evidence Boundaries & Simulation Model Grounding

### Documented Claims Permitted in Script
- **Fairness / gap reduction:** Opening salaries sharply narrows hidden pay gaps between people doing the same job (Bennedsen et al. 2022; Baker et al. 2023; Obloj & Zenger 2022).
- **Immediate asymmetric shock:** People peek mostly at peers in the same department/circle; those paid below peers lose satisfaction, cut effort, and start job-hunting, while those above median don't get happier; rich-vs-poor happiness gap widens (Card et al. 2012; Cullen & Perez-Truglia 2022; Perez-Truglia 2020).
- **Employer bargaining-power trap (3D cutaway core):** Under secrecy, HR can quietly raise one standout worker; under open slips, raising one person pressures HR to raise every peer in the same role, so companies cap raises and flatten salary bands (Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson 2023).
- **Flatter merit rewards & top exits:** Pay becomes more equal, but less tied to individual performance, average wages can stagnate/edge down slightly, and high performers/top managers quit more often or chase non-salary perks/new titles (Obloj & Zenger 2022; Mas 2017; Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson 2023; Baker et al. 2023).
- **Decisive flip condition (100-world split):** Whether World A outperforms World B hinges on **cross-firm job mobility & clear performance rules** — when workers can easily jump to higher-paying competitors, transparency fuels wage competition; when switching is hard, employers lock wages flat (Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson 2023; Arnold et al. 2025).

### Claims Explicitly Excluded (Avoid Overclaiming)
- Do **not** claim open salaries *always* crash wages in every economy (cross-firm competition and unionized settings behave differently).
- Do **not** claim open salaries make *every* worker miserable (underpaid workers genuinely gain fairer pay, and learning *managers'* pay can motivate effort when promotion paths are clear).
- Do **not** present our 10-year twin-world model numbers (e.g., 100,000 workers, Year 1–10 fairness vs. average-wage trajectories, or 54/46 100-world split) as real-world official statistics — frame them explicitly as model simulation outputs (`Eksperimen pikiran. Angka dari model.`).
