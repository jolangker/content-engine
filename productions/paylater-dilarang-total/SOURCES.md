## Verified Empirical Sources & Claim Mapping

### 1. BNPL & Installments Drive Retail Volume ("Liquidity Flypaper Effect")
- **Source:** Marco Di Maggio, Justin Katz, & Emily Williams (2022), *"Buy Now, Pay Later Credit: User Characteristics and Effects on Spending Patterns"*, National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) Working Paper No. 30508.
- **Direct URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w30508
- **Documented findings:**
  - Access to point-of-sale BNPL installment loans increases total consumer spending and raises the retail share of spending across both liquidity-constrained and unconstrained consumers ("liquidity flypaper effect" — retail liquidity sticks where it hits, with an estimated persistent bump of ~$60/week in retail spending).
- **Episode claim mapping:**
  - **S05 (Honeymoon):** Removing paylater & installments immediately drops impulsive retail spending (`"belanja impulsif anjlok"`).
  - **S08–S09 (Retail slowdown):** Conversely, removing installment financing sharply contracts retail demand in electronics, furniture, and small-merchant sales, reducing courier parcel volume (`"toko Dunia A sepi"`, `"Penjualan elektronik dan perabot merosot tajam"`).
- **Qualification:** Study examines US consumer transaction data; our simulation applies the general behavioral mechanism to a stylized twin-city thought experiment rather than claiming an exact Indonesian percentage.

### 2. Embedded BNPL Expands Merchant Activity & Access for Liquidity-Constrained Buyers
- **Source:** Wenlong Bian, Lin William Cong, & Yang Ji (2023; revised 2024), *"The Rise of E-Wallets and Buy-Now-Pay-Later: Payment Competition, Credit Expansion, and Consumer Behavior"* (NBER Working Paper No. 31202) / *"The Rise of E-Wallet Super-Apps and Buy-Now-Pay-Later"* (NBER Working Paper No. 33178).
- **Direct URLs:**
  - https://www.nber.org/papers/w31202
  - https://www.nber.org/papers/w33178
- **Documented findings:**
  - Embedded e-wallet BNPL expands credit access along the extensive margin to underserved and liquidity-constrained consumers, acts as a payment/credit lubricant across online and on-site retail, and significantly lifts consumer expenditure and merchant shopping activity without necessarily triggering runaway defaults when properly disciplined.
- **Episode claim mapping:**
  - **S10 & S13–S14 (Cash-wall mechanism):** Splitting large durable/work-tool prices into manageable slices lets cash-constrained workers purchase needed appliances/gear and keeps shops and couriers moving; banning all installments puts up a hard cash wall blocking both impulsive shoppers and workers with genuine needs.

### 3. Loan Stacking & Household Debt Stress Under Unchecked Paylater
- **Source:** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) (2023 & 2025), *"Consumer Use of Buy Now, Pay Later"* / *Making Ends Meet* BNPL Research Series.
- **Direct URL:** https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/consumer-use-of-buy-now-pay-later/
- **Documented findings:**
  - Roughly **63%** of BNPL borrowers held multiple simultaneous loans during the year, and about **one-third (33%)** stacked loans across multiple providers simultaneously.
  - BNPL users display significantly higher rates of delinquency on traditional credit products and higher credit-card utilization (60–66%) than non-users, demonstrating real debt-stacking stress in the unregulated/permissive World B status quo.
- **Episode claim mapping:**
  - **S06–S07 & S16:** Grounds why World A enjoys genuine early relief (fewer stacked monthly bills, calmer sleep, impulsive shoppers protected) and why World B is not painless (citizens stacking 3–4 simultaneous installments).

### 4. Restricting Formal Short-Term Credit Pushes Constrained Households Into Worse Substitutes
- **Source:** Jonathan Zinman (2010), *"Restricting consumer credit access: Household survey evidence on effects around the Oregon rate cap"*, *Journal of Banking & Finance*, Vol. 34, Issue 3, pp. 546–556.
- **Direct URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2009.08.024
- **Documented findings:**
  - Following Oregon's binding restriction on short-term consumer loans, former borrowers did not painlessly absorb shocks via cash savings; instead, they shifted partially into plausibly inferior substitutes including bank overdrafts and late bill payments, with overall household financial condition deteriorating relative to unaffected Washington households.
- **Episode claim mapping:**
  - **S11 & S14–S15:** Explains why banning formal installments does not eliminate underlying household liquidity shocks and contributes to World A's Year 2–3 reversal (`Berbalik!`).

### 5. Outright Credit Bans Shift Borrowers to Pawnshops & High-Cost Informal Alternatives
- **Source:** Neil Bhutta, Jacob Goldin, & Tatiana Homonoff (2016), *"Consumer Borrowing after Payday Loan Bans"*, *Journal of Law and Economics*, Vol. 59, No. 1, pp. 225–259.
- **Direct URL:** https://doi.org/10.1086/686033
- **Documented findings:**
  - State bans on payday loans reduce formal short-term borrowing, *but* borrowers do not migrate to cheap mainstream credit; instead, a substantial share substitutes into alternative high-cost financial services such as pawnshop loans.
- **Episode claim mapping:**
  - **S11, S14 & S17:** Directly supports the concrete visual of cash-strapped World A workers turning toward pawnshops and unregulated back-alley lenders (`gadai dan pinjaman gelap`), and grounds the 100-world flip condition: banning consumer paylater only produces a net win when paired with affordable emergency/productive credit (`kredit darurat`).

## Illustrative Simulation Parameters (Explicitly Framed as Model Outcomes)

- **Horizon:** 10 years (`Tahun 0–10`) comparing twin cities `Dunia A` (total ban on consumer installments & paylater; cash-only retail) vs `Dunia B` (standard regulated installments & paylater permitted).
- **Archetype:** **Archetype B — *Enak di awal, bumerang di akhir* (Honeymoon → Backfire)**.
  - **Years 0–1:** `Dunia A` rises cleanly above `Dunia B` on household financial calm & reduced impulsive debt (`+12` index lead at Month 6–12).
  - **Year 2–3 (`Berbalik!`):** Retail contraction, small-shop closures, courier job loss, and informal-lending migration pull `Dunia A` below `Dunia B` around Year 3.
  - **100-World Robustness Split (`S17`):** `68 / 100` won by `Dunia B`, `32 / 100` won by `Dunia A` (specifically the subset of model runs where affordable emergency/productive credit and household savings buffers replace banned retail paylater).
