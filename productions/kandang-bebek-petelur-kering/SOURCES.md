(2022), *Water for Domestic Ducks: The Benefits and Challenges in Commercial Production*, Frontiers in Animal Science, 3:782507 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/animal-science/articles/10.3389/fanim.2022.782507/full | Peer-reviewed review (27 Jan 2022) | Ducks are semi-aquatic waterfowl; open water allowing head/nostril immersion (≥8 cm depth in experimental troughs; Liste et al. 2012b) maintains eye, nostril, and feather cleanliness, whereas nipple-only access can impair eye/nostril hygiene or cause rebound bathing behavior. Higher litter moisture worsens footpad condition (footpad dermatitis) and plumage cleanliness. Predation is a primary risk in outdoor/semi-outdoor flocks. Natural or supplemental lighting maintains photoperiod for laying (~14 h light). |

## Claim-to-evidence mapping

| Claim | Episode usage | Supporting sources | Qualifications & boundaries |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **C1 — Dry-litter laying coop feasibility & density** | A backyard keeper can raise ~12 ready-to-lay local ducks (*bebek petelur / bayah* ~5 months) in a ~2 × 2 m (4 m²) dry-litter coop without a swimming pond (~3 ducks/m²). | S4, S5, S9, S10 | Intensive dry housing requires daily feed, fresh water, and litter upkeep (S10); ducks accustomed to wet ponds may need gradual adaptation (S10). Stocking density must stay uncrowded (≤3–4 ducks/m²) to avoid rapid wetting and stress (S5, S10). |
| **C2 — Water splash vs head-dipping welfare** | Ducks splash open bowls into mud, yet still need water deep enough (≥8 cm) to dip their bills/nostrils to keep eyes and nostrils clean. Placing a covered/hole-cut deep drinker over a slatted platform and gravel soak drain keeps the main floor dry without depriving head immersion. | S1, S2, S3, S11 | Do not claim ducks need zero open water or recommend nipple-only systems as superior for welfare (S1, S2). Frame the covered deep bucket/trough over slats + gravel drain as an illustrative backyard layout combining head-dipping access with splash drainage (S3). |
| **C3 — Midday heat stress & roof/ventilation** | Ducks have no sweat glands and pant with open bills under hot sun; a slanted shade roof with cross-ventilation gaps lowers radiant heat and evacuates warm, humid air. | S1, S6, S11 | Shading and airflow mitigate solar/metabolic heat load; in very hot tropical middays ducks still need cool fresh water (S1, S6). |
| **C4 — Wet droppings, ammonia & dry rice-husk litter** | Accumulated wet droppings generate ammonia odor and irritate feet; a dry rice-husk (*sekam padi*) litter layer with regular turning and removal of damp clumps absorbs moisture and cuts odor. | S2, S4, S7, S11 | Optimal litter moisture is ~20–25% (S7); rice husks are not maintenance-free — wet spots must be scooped and replaced, and airflow must stay open (S4, S7). |
| **C5 — Driving rain, runoff & perimeter drainage** | Wind-driven rain and yard runoff quickly saturate dry litter; roof eaves, rollable side tarps/curtains, and an exterior perimeter drain trench keep rainwater out. | S6, S11 | Illustrative yard drainage layout; exact curtain material and trench depth depend on local rainfall and yard slope. |
| **C6 — Morning laying & floor-level nest boxes** | Laying ducks typically lay between ~03:00 and 08:00; without dry sheltered spots, eggs scatter on the floor and get soiled or cracked. Floor-level nest boxes (~30 × 30 cm, ~1 per 4–5 ducks, bedded with dry straw/husks — no elevated perches) keep morning eggs clean and intact. | S2, S8 | Ducks lay at floor level and do not use high chicken perches/nests (S8); a few birds may still lay outside nests until habituated. |
| **C7 — Feed spillage, trough design & water placement** | Ducks scatter shallow/open mash into damp ground, attracting flies and wasting feed; a deep lipped trough set slightly apart from the water drain (still accessible for drinking while eating) cuts spills. | S9, S11 | Ducks need nearby water to wash down feed easily (S1, S3), so separate the splash zone physically without isolating water far from feed. |
| **C8 — Night predators & calm dim night lighting** | Rats and stray cats raid feed and startle ducks at night; burying/wrapping fine wire mesh along the lower perimeter plus a soft dim night bulb keeps predators out and reduces night-time panic/disruption. | S2, S6, S11 | Note mixed local practice on night lighting (e.g., S8 notes one farm avoided bright lights that drew bats); frame the bulb specifically as a soft, dim warm light inside a wire-enclosed coop to keep the flock calm and photoperiod steady (S2, S6). |
| **C9 — First weekly egg yield & dated gross value estimate** | 12 stabilized local laying ducks (at ~65–70% average HDP, ~8–9 eggs/day; S9) produce roughly **60 eggs (2 standard 30-egg trays)** over one week. At **Rp3.500/butir** (traditional market average across 5 markets in Demak, Central Java, Dindagkop UKM survey Sep 2026, S12), gross value is **≈ Rp210.000** (`estimasi harga pasar / Sep 2026`). | S8, S9, S12, S13 | **Gross estimate only — never profit, monthly income, or payback.** Retail/traditional-market price (Rp3.500/butir, Sep 2026, S12) applies to direct local/market sales; wholesale collector/farm-gate prices can be substantially lower and fluctuate sharply (Rp1.400–Rp2.200/butir reported in May 2026, S13), and feed/pullet costs are excluded. |
