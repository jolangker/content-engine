# Scam Autopsy

## The viewer

A text arrives while someone is making dinner. The first question is whether to answer. Give US and UK adults aged 35–65 a recognisable detail they can use at that moment. An adult child should be able to forward the clip to a parent without explaining it or implying that the parent was foolish. English narration and burned-in English captions throughout.

## Commissioning an episode

Name one currently circulating scam and the country covered by the evidence. Choose a dated FTC, Action Fraud, bank or carrier advisory that describes that variant. Read the body, follow any replacement notice and check current relevance at production time. An older advisory can explain the mechanism when recent reporting independently confirms circulation. A US report supports a US claim. Verify separate UK evidence before claiming local prevalence or giving a UK reporting route.

| Pillar | Candidate cases | Artifact to build for this episode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Message scams | Wrong-number texts, parcel or toll smishing, fake two-factor authentication alerts | A short thread, cropped delivery notice or masked verification prompt |
| Money movement | Relationship investment fraud (often called “pig butchering”), marketplace overpayment, fake crypto or job tasks | A missing payout, an overpayment entry or a task balance beside an out-of-pocket payment |
| Tech and identity traps | QR phishing, AI voice-clone calls, SIM swaps | A partly peeled sticker, call transcript fragment or service-loss notice |

These are assignments to research. For each episode commission an original recipient-side artifact, its turning point and its loss diagram. Change the message fragment, context, crop and highlighted evidence. Only the design rules and four questions recur.

## Four beats

| Beat | Question for the editor | Wrong-number example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The bait | What ordinary thing has just appeared? Show the object on frame zero. | A lunch text addressed to Nina lands on someone else's phone. |
| The hook moment | Why might a reasonable person stay? Show the social or emotional turn. | The recipient corrects the mistake. The stranger still wants to talk. |
| Where the money leaves | At what point does the person part with money or control? Draw the victim-side consequence. | Later a supposed investment comes up. A payment to that bogus platform goes to the scammer. Receiving the text itself costs the viewer nothing in this example. |
| The one tell | What single observable detail can someone recall tomorrow? Return to its exact place on the artifact. | The stranger keeps the chat going after the mix-up is clear. Leave the unexpected text unanswered. |

Where the source documents identity loss without a transfer, label what changes hands and say money is *at risk* if that follows from the source. Never invent a debit. Speak as a calm observer beside the recipient. Prefer “they already know you’re not Nina” to a general warning to “stay vigilant.”

## Platform cuts

Produce a 1080 × 1920, 30 fps, 1× master for each edit. Show subject motion strictly before 1 s and deliver the hook within 0–3 s. Keep captions burned in through both cuts.

- **YouTube Shorts / Reels, 35–45 s.** A 40 s example spends 0–7 s on the bait (opening hook by 3 s), 7–16 s on the social turn, 16–29 s following the loss and 29–40 s identifying the tell and a safe action.
- **TikTok, 61–75 s.** A 67 s example holds evidence longer across the first four beats (0–9, 9–22, 22–36, 36–48 s). Add a fifth beat at 48–67 s, “What to do if it already happened.” Tailor it to the actual harm. For a transfer, contact the bank or payment provider promptly through a trusted channel, explain the payment and ask what can be done. Check the source for the relevant country and payment method. Avoid promising recovery.

The contract schema has one duration range. Its outer bounds admit both formats. The mandatory `cut-length` verdict checks the measured runtime against the named platform, including the gap between cuts. Record platform, runtime and recovery beat where applicable in the episode review.

## Evidence on screen

Keep the issuer and publication date readable beside the first supported claim. Add the short advisory title and section where useful. The episode source log records the original title, date, country, URL, supported claim and any uncertainty. Give viewers the full reference in delivery copy. A dateline on the fictional message cannot stand in for the advisory date.

## Safe reconstruction

Show recognition cues from the recipient’s point of view. Label fabricated screens as reconstructions. Use fictional brands, reserved fictional phone numbers and inert interface fragments. Omit genuine customer data, live destinations, usable QR payloads, credentials, dialing instructions and complete persuasive exchanges. Identify a red flag with a ring and explanatory words so it remains legible without colour. Use the real issuer’s name in a separate source credit. Disclose synthetic narration wherever the platform or local rules require it and record the disclosure at delivery. The pilot carries a visible AI narration credit.
