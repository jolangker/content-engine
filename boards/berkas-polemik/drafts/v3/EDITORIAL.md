## Purpose and audience experience

Investigate dark chapters of Indonesian history and conspiracies in Indonesia: violence, repression, covert coordination, struggles for power, and consequential events with contested explanations. Source the account through historical books, scholarly journal articles, and other credible, traceable material. Serve Indonesian-speaking general viewers, including young adults curious about the actors, interests, mechanisms, and human consequences behind these events.

Accepted audience-facing promise: **“Berkas Polemik membedah sejarah kelam dan dugaan konspirasi di Indonesia lewat penelusuran sumber.”**

Core source base: books, journals, and documents. Daily political/current-affairs reporting is not an account pillar. It was discussed as a possible expansion and then set aside when the user accepted returning to the focused historical identity. A current event may motivate a historically grounded episode, but does not qualify as a standalone news topic under this board. Do not add a news pillar or a history/news posting ratio without an explicit change of direction.

Each episode should leave the viewer with one consequential question, a source-supported finding, and the most important remaining uncertainty. Build tension through stakes, competing interests, sequence, and what the evidence actually reveals. A documented conspiracy can be explained as such; a suspected plot can be investigated without prejudging its conclusion. A dark-history episode does not need a conspiracy angle to qualify.

General historical trivia, spelling changes, or document curiosities are outside the main selection priority unless they materially illuminate the board's dark-history or conspiracy focus. Do not select a minor curiosity solely because an accessible source makes it easy to produce.

## Account voice

A patient archival researcher: precise, curious, clear about uncertainty, and attentive to people affected by the events. Calm delivery need not sound emotionally indifferent to suffering. Explain technical terms at first use. Use direct, natural Indonesian; avoid prosecutorial certainty and theatrical secrecy.

| Prefer | Avoid unless the evidence specifically supports it |
| --- | --- |
| “Dua sumber ini mencatat hal berbeda.” | “Sejarah yang sengaja disembunyikan.” |
| “Laporan ini menyebut…” | Treating a report's allegation as an established event. |
| “Bagian ini belum dapat dipastikan.” | “Kebenaran akhirnya terbongkar.” |
| “Catatan ini menunjukkan keputusan berubah.” | Claiming a secret motive from a changed decision alone. |

These are illustrative wording patterns, not historical claims.

## Five topic pillars

| Pillar | Reusable question | Useful source families |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Violence and repression | How did a dark event unfold, who was affected, and which institutions or actors are supported by the evidence? | Historical monographs, journal articles, investigative or inquiry reports, survivor testimony with context. |
| Covert operations and struggles for power | What coordination, mechanism, or objective can be established, and which parts remain alleged? | Scholarly histories, investigative books with transparent sourcing, correspondence, operational and judicial records where available. |
| Disappearances, killings, and unresolved cases | What is established about the event, which explanations have evidentiary support, and what remains unresolved? | Relevant scholarship, sourced investigative books, case or commission records, contemporaneous reporting. |
| Foreign involvement and hidden interests | What evidence supports an outside actor's involvement or influence in an Indonesian event? | Historical books, journal research, diplomatic and declassified records read in context. |
| Competing narratives and conspiracy claims | How did a consequential explanation arise, what supports it, and what contradicts or limits it? | Books or articles presenting the claim, independent scholarly analysis, and the underlying evidence when accessible. |

Choose a pillar when the source base supports an answer. Do not require every episode to contain two opposing camps. Compare evidentiary weight, not equal screen time for every assertion.

## Handling conspiracy as a subject

Distinguish these states in research and audience-facing language:

- **Documented coordination or plot:** describe only the actors, actions, and purposes supported by the cited evidence; do not extrapolate to a larger unseen network.
- **A source-backed allegation or disputed explanation:** identify who makes the claim, its supporting evidence, relevant competing scholarship, and its unresolved elements.
- **A circulating conspiracy narrative:** investigate the narrative's origin and evidentiary status. Popular repetition is not independent corroboration.

The account may investigate all three. Do not force every story into either automatic debunking or automatic confirmation. Names, accusations, intent, and causal links require precise attribution. Keep the narrative distinction between a demonstrated fact, a source's claim, scholarly interpretation, and speculation visible throughout the episode.

## Source base: books, journals, and corroborating material

Books and journals are core research sources, not decorative citations added after a script is written. A production need not discover a new primary archive to qualify: serious scholarship may provide a sound basis for a bounded explanation.

| Material | What to inspect and record |
| --- | --- |
| Historical or scholarly book | Author, title, edition, publisher, year, exact chapter/pages, methodology, and the evidence supporting the selected passage. |
| Scholarly journal article | Author, article and journal titles, year, volume/issue, pages, DOI or stable URL where available, methods, and scope. Check whether it actually supports the episode's claim. |
| Sourced investigative book or long-form investigation | Author expertise, reporting methods, named/anonymous source limitations, references, and independent corroboration for serious allegations. |
| Thesis or dissertation | University/repository, author, year, page references, methods, and relevance; assess the work rather than treating the document label as an automatic guarantee. |
| Archive, commission report, official inquiry, court record, or contemporaneous press | Provenance, date, institutional perspective, access status, and what the record can establish. An official origin does not by itself settle disputed claims. |
| Memoir or witness testimony | Relationship to the event, recording date, perspective and memory limits, and corroboration where the claim requires it. |

Use searches and web summaries to discover sources. Inspect the actual relevant pages or article passages before quoting or treating them as read. A catalogue entry or abstract can establish a lead, not the contents of inaccessible pages. Record access limits, avoid invented page numbers, and do not claim to have read a book from snippets.

For a contested causal explanation or serious allegation, normally seek at least two substantively independent sources, including relevant book or journal scholarship. Several publications repeating the same testimony do not become several independent evidentiary trails. A single-source explanation must be attributed and bounded; if it cannot support the intended angle, narrow the question or choose a different candidate.

## The dossier structure

**User-confirmed format: approximately 90 seconds with exactly 16 primary storyboard frames/scenes.** Each scene has one dominant visual and little on-screen copy. The mean duration is 5.625 seconds; vary individual holds according to the evidence, narration, and reading needs. Reduce scope before accelerating delivery. Keep one core question per episode.

The following 90-second allocation is a reusable planning template. The duration/count are confirmed; the particular allocation is an implementation proposal that can adapt to each researched episode.

| Function | Time | Scene numbers | Count | What the viewer receives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question / hook | 0–10 s | 01–02 | 2 | A visual discrepancy and the core question. |
| Source and context | 10–27 s | 03–05 | 3 | Source identity, relevant people/place/time, and why the question matters. |
| Read and compare evidence | 27–63 s | 06–11 | 6 | Source objects, readable details, comparison, and corroboration with context preserved. |
| Test the interpretation | 63–81 s | 12–14 | 3 | The link between evidence and inference, competing explanation where relevant, and a meaningful limit. |
| Finding and limit | 81–90 s | 15–16 | 2 | The supported finding followed by its remaining limit. |
| **Total** | **90 s** | **01–16** | **16** | |

Illustrative scene durations in seconds, in order: **4, 6, 5, 6, 6, 5, 7, 6, 6, 5, 7, 5, 6, 7, 5, 4**. These sum to 90 seconds. A detail change inside a scene is not an additional primary scene unless it becomes a separately numbered storyboard composition. Transitions fit within the 90-second timeline; do not append them as extra runtime.

The three style-board studies are independent design examples, not the complete 16-scene episode. Build the actual sixteen scene compositions during an explicitly requested episode production. Permit a documentary close reading, a comparison, or a chronology to lead when the material calls for it.

End with substance before an optional discussion question. An unresolved issue does not erase what is known; a well-supported finding need not end in artificial doubt. Do not ask viewers to vote on an allegation as a substitute for evidence.

## Source and interpretation rules

1. Maintain a production claim ledger linking each substantive claim to the relevant page, passage, timestamp, or catalogue record. Record access date and any access limitation. A promising topic is not a verified episode.
2. Separate the authenticity/provenance of a record from the reliability of what it asserts. A primary source establishes what its author recorded; its contents may require corroboration. An official, colonial, diplomatic, or intelligence origin does not automatically settle the event described.
3. Identify original, copy, transcription, translation, later recollection, and editorial reconstruction accurately. Do not invent archival identifiers. Inaccessible material can be described as reported by a named secondary source, not as personally inspected.
4. Use relevant scholarship to establish context and interpret disagreement. Assess expertise, argument, evidence, and relevance to the question; a name on a preferred-author list is not sufficient validation.
5. Use quotation marks only for verified words. Preserve wording and meaning, mark omissions, identify the speaker/author, and retain locator information. Label translations and editorial additions. A paraphrase must not resemble a verbatim quotation.
6. Distinguish observation, a source's allegation, an interpretation, and an inference. Attribute claims about motives or intent. Missing records alone do not establish deliberate destruction, concealment, or a particular perpetrator.
7. Reconstruct a chronology only at the resolution the sources support. Distinguish documented times, approximate times, and disputed sequences; never invent minute-by-minute precision for tension.
8. Keep historical suffering and coercion in view. An administrative payment entry alone does not establish that workers received pay or disprove coercion. Do not simplify violence into a clever archival twist.
9. Place concise source identification on screen while the evidence is discussed; retain full references and limitations in the production package and accompanying public source list. Avoid placeholder source codes in a published episode.
10. Make corrections traceable. Record the changed claim, its evidence, and which script/visual/package versions are affected.

## Visual truth and presentation

- A scanned source, editorial transcription, paraphrase, and reconstruction use visibly different labels.
- A manila card is a presentation surface, not proof that its text is an original record.
- Use “RAHASIA”, “DEKLASIFIKASI”, or institutional verification labels only when the source's documented status justifies them. Series graphics must not imply official endorsement.
- A mask used to focus attention is labelled editorial emphasis. Do not present it as historical censorship. Represent actual source redactions only with their context intact.
- The static board's examples are clearly marked **“SIMULASI · BUKAN ARSIP”** and cannot be carried into production as historical evidence.

## Topic intake and readiness

For automatic selection, first gate candidates by the confirmed niche: the topic must materially concern dark Indonesian history or a consequential conspiracy/plot narrative in Indonesia. Within that scope, compare:

The default is the focused historical/investigative identity, not a general political-news channel. Select a consequential event, a substantive question, and a traceable evidence path. The discussion of current regime-related news does not authorize broadening this gate.

1. Historical/human stakes and a clear reason the audience should care.
2. A substantive question about actors, interests, mechanisms, or conflicting explanations.
3. The finding or understanding an episode could actually deliver.
4. A credible source trail through books/journals and other appropriate material, with passage-level access or explicit limitations.
5. Novelty against existing episodes and a bounded angle feasible in approximately 90 seconds / sixteen scenes.
6. Meaningful visual relationships that can develop the explanation without padding sixteen scenes with repeated claims.

Evidence availability is a viability gate, not a reason to prefer an otherwise weak or out-of-scope curiosity. Do not require a current trend or claim current popularity without verification. Produce a shortlist and select within the niche; if no candidate has a sufficient evidentiary path, report that limitation instead of silently falling back to generic history trivia.

For each viable candidate, record the pillar, audience question, stakes, candidate angle, book/article leads, which material was actually inspected, the smallest supported answer, and the principal uncertainty. Investigate the v1 topic list as leads only; its document names, quotations, figures, and implied explanations have not been validated by this refinement. The completed `penutup-proklamasi` episode is a technical dry run, not a model for the revised topic priority.

Proceed to an episode only after the research supports its angle. If a record cannot be obtained, narrow the question or explain the limitation. Board work alone does not authorize or create an episode.
