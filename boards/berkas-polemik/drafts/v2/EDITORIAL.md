# Berkas Polemik — editorial proposal v2

Status: awaiting review. This proposal does not supersede the approved v1 framework at the board root.

## Purpose and audience experience

Explain contested Indonesian history by examining traceable sources and the reasoning connecting them to a conclusion. Serve Indonesian-speaking general viewers, including young adults who want context beyond a familiar summary. Assume curiosity rather than specialist vocabulary.

Audience-facing promise: **“Menguji polemik sejarah dari jejak yang bisa ditelusuri.”**

Each episode should leave the viewer with one supported finding, the evidence behind it, and the most consequential remaining limit. A correction, confirmation, or unresolved question can each be a worthwhile result. Controversy is a topic selection lens, not evidence of a conspiracy.

## Account voice

A patient archival researcher: precise, curious, clear about uncertainty, and attentive to people affected by the events. Calm delivery need not sound emotionally indifferent to suffering. Explain technical terms at first use. Use direct, natural Indonesian; avoid prosecutorial certainty and theatrical secrecy.

| Prefer | Avoid unless the evidence specifically supports it |
| --- | --- |
| “Dua sumber ini mencatat hal berbeda.” | “Sejarah yang sengaja disembunyikan.” |
| “Laporan ini menyebut…” | Treating a report's allegation as an established event. |
| “Bagian ini belum dapat dipastikan.” | “Kebenaran akhirnya terbongkar.” |
| “Catatan ini menunjukkan keputusan berubah.” | Claiming a secret motive from a changed decision alone. |

These are illustrative wording patterns, not historical claims.

## Five topic pillars

| Pillar | Reusable question | Useful source families |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document trails | What survives, in which version, and with what provenance? | Catalogues, copies, correspondence, acquisition records. |
| Popular accounts under examination | Which part of a familiar account is supported, simplified, or disputed? | Period records and relevant historical scholarship. |
| Decisions and competing ideas | What did participants argue, and which constraints shaped their choices? | Minutes, speeches, pamphlets, correspondence. |
| Indonesia in international records | What did outside observers know, claim, or try to influence? | Diplomatic records, declassified reports, domestic corroboration. |
| Critical turning points | What can be reconstructed about a consequential sequence? | Timestamped records, broadcasts, diaries, independently checked testimony. |

Choose a pillar when the source base supports an answer. Do not require every episode to contain two opposing camps. Compare evidentiary weight, not equal screen time for every assertion.

## The dossier structure

Default length: 60–90 seconds, subject to the episode's actual narration and reading needs. One core question per episode; reduce scope before accelerating delivery.

| Function | Illustrative 75-second allocation | What the viewer receives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | 0–6 s | A concrete discrepancy or consequential question, without a promised scandal. |
| Source and context | 6–18 s | Who produced the material, when, and what kind of record it is. |
| Read the evidence | 18–40 s | One or two legible excerpts or details, with context preserved. |
| Test the interpretation | 40–60 s | Corroboration, a meaningful competing explanation, or an evidence limitation. |
| Finding and limit | 60–75 s | The strongest supported conclusion and what remains unresolved. |

This allocation is an example, not a rigid template. Permit a documentary close reading, a comparison, or a chronology to lead when the material calls for it.

End with substance before an optional discussion question. An unresolved issue does not erase what is known; a well-supported finding need not end in artificial doubt. Do not ask viewers to vote on an allegation as a substitute for evidence.

## Source and interpretation rules

1. Maintain a production claim ledger linking each substantive claim to the relevant page, passage, timestamp, or catalogue record. Record access date and any access limitation. A promising topic is not a verified episode.
2. Separate the authenticity/provenance of a record from the reliability of what it asserts. A primary source establishes what its author recorded; its contents may require corroboration. An official, colonial, diplomatic, or intelligence origin does not automatically settle the event described.
3. Identify original, copy, transcription, translation, later recollection, and editorial reconstruction accurately. Do not invent archival identifiers. Inaccessible material can be described as reported by a named secondary source, not as personally inspected.
4. Use relevant scholarship to establish context and interpret disagreement. Assess expertise, argument, evidence, and relevance to the question; a name on a preferred-author list is not sufficient validation.
5. Use quotation marks only for verified words. Preserve wording and meaning, mark omissions, identify the speaker/author, and retain locator information. Label translations and editorial additions. A paraphrase must not resemble a verbatim quotation.
6. Distinguish observation, a source's allegation, an interpretation, and an inference. Attribute claims about motives or intent. Missing records alone do not establish deliberate destruction, concealment, or a particular perpetrator.
7. Reconstruct a chronology only at the resolution the sources support. Distinguish documented times, approximate times, and disputed sequences; never invent minute-by-minute precision for tension.
8. Keep historical suffering and coercion in view. An administrative payment entry alone does not establish that workers received pay or disprove coercion. Do not simplify violence into a clever archival twist.
9. Place concise source identification on screen while the evidence is discussed; retain full references and limitations in the production package and accompanying public source list. Avoid placeholder source codes in a published episode.
10. Make corrections traceable. Record the changed claim, its evidence, and which script/visual/package versions are affected.

## Visual truth and presentation

- A scanned source, editorial transcription, paraphrase, and reconstruction use visibly different labels.
- A manila card is a presentation surface, not proof that its text is an original record.
- Use “RAHASIA”, “DEKLASIFIKASI”, or institutional verification labels only when the source's documented status justifies them. Series graphics must not imply official endorsement.
- A mask used to focus attention is labelled editorial emphasis. Do not present it as historical censorship. Represent actual source redactions only with their context intact.
- The static board's examples are clearly marked **“SIMULASI · BUKAN ARSIP”** and cannot be carried into production as historical evidence.

## Topic intake and readiness

For each candidate, write a neutral question, the available source trail, a relevant scholarly context source, the smallest useful answer, and the principal uncertainty. Investigate the v1 topic list as leads only; its document names, quotations, figures, and implied explanations have not been validated by this refinement.

Proceed to an episode only after the research supports its angle. If a record cannot be obtained, narrow the question or explain the limitation. Board work alone does not authorize or create an episode.
