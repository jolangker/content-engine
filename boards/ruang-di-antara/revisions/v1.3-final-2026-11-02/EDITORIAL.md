## Purpose and voice

Help Indonesian adults recognize an everyday relationship experience, understand a relevant psychology concept through the interaction and consider one concrete response or observation. The voice is an intimate, observant reflection companion; the substance explains a pattern. Use natural conversational Indonesian with lo/lu–gua speech, consistent within each episode. Warmth comes from specific details and fair descriptions of actions and their effects.

## Scope and pillars

| Pillar | Main question | Example direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Within oneself | What do I notice, feel and do here? | Wanting closeness while holding back |
| Between us | How do our responses form an interaction? | One asks to talk; the other requests time |
| Event and interpretation | What happened, and what meaning was added? | A short reply and the assumptions around it |
| Needs and boundaries | What needs to be expressed or agreed? | Privacy, support, time alone |
| After conflict | What needs understanding, follow-through or repair? | Returning to an unfinished conversation |

Pillars guide planning and do not need on-screen labels. Include positive everyday connection, curiosity and shared enjoyment alongside tension and conflict.

## Recognizable, varied question hooks — user requirement

Open through a specific relationship moment the viewer can recognize, with a question the episode will answer. A brief observation or quoted dialogue may lead into that question. Establish the situation and emotional friction immediately. Avoid generic opening questions that could introduce any topic, stacked questions, blame disguised as a question or an implied diagnosis.

Vary the entry point, viewpoint and framing, not just synonyms for "Pernah nggak". Do not make "Pernah nggak sih lo/lu..." the recurring opening formula. Available entry points include an experience, a puzzling response, quoted dialogue, an internal conflict or a small observable detail. Check recent scripts on this board for repetitive openings when choosing a new hook.

Fictional alternatives for the same situation, not separate mandatory script beats or researched claims:

| Entry point | Illustrative hook |
| --- | --- |
| Recognized experience | “Lu pernah selesai cerita, tapi malah ngerasa belum didengerin?” |
| Puzzling response | “Kok udah dikasih solusi, lu malah makin males cerita?” |
| Everyday dialogue | “‘Ya udah, cari kerja lain aja.’ Kenapa jawaban ini kadang bikin cerita langsung berhenti?” |
| Internal conflict | “Lu tahu dia niat bantu. Terus kenapa responsnya tetap bikin ganjel?” |
| Observable detail | “Baru dua kalimat lu cerita, sarannya udah panjang. Bagian beratnya sempat keluar belum?” |

Deliver the recognizable question through narration and full captions. Main emphasis copy can show a short question such as “Cuma mau cerita?” or successive phrases. It does not need to repeat the whole spoken question. By the end, the viewer should understand the question more precisely through the concept and have an observation, distinction or response relevant to it.

## Bridges and concept introduction

Write the hook and bridge as one connected opening. The bridge explains why this concept is relevant to the situation. Introduce the concept early, after enough context to make the connection understandable; it can arrive in the second or third sentence rather than immediately after the question. Avoid a long scene or advice sequence before the concept is introduced.

Vary the bridge according to the reasoning required:

- Shift a possible interpretation: “Mungkin yang lu butuhin saat itu bukan jawaban dulu, tapi kesempatan buat menyelesaikan cerita.”
- Distinguish two things: “Niat membantu dan membuat orang merasa dipahami belum tentu berjalan bareng.”
- Ask a connected follow-up: “Sebelum kasih saran, dia udah tahu belum bagian mana yang paling berat buat lu?”
- Lead through an action: “Ada langkah sebelum menyusun solusi: pahami dulu ceritanya, lalu cek tangkapan kita.”
- Introduce the concept directly when the connection is already clear: “Di sini ada konsep active listening, atau mendengarkan aktif.”

These are illustrative copy patterns, not established explanations of every viewer's situation. Use “mungkin bukan... tapi...” only when a defensible alternative interpretation matters. Do not turn it into another recurring formula or use “mungkin” to excuse an unsupported claim. Choose each bridge for its fit with the episode rather than rotating templates mechanically.

State the concept's established **English name**, immediately give its **Indonesian meaning**, and explain it in everyday Indonesian. For example: “Active listening, atau mendengarkan aktif. Artinya, lu berusaha memahami cerita seseorang, lalu mengecek apakah yang lu tangkap udah tepat.” Introduce the term once and use ordinary language afterward. Narrate the English name and Indonesian meaning; retaining them only in emphasis copy does not satisfy this requirement.

Use “itu namanya...” only when the described experience is actually an example of the named concept. When a concept helps interpret the scene without naming the experience itself, use wording such as “ini berkaitan dengan...” or “ada konsep yang relevan di sini...”. Do not label someone, imply a diagnosis or invent a scientific term to fit a hook. Verify the term and its applicability during episode research before locking the script.

## Narrative reasoning

Use this reasoning path: **recognizable hook → bridge → English concept name and Indonesian meaning → plain-language explanation → substantive discussion through the scene → concrete application → closing answer**. Keep one concept, situation and main question. These are connected narrative functions, not equal blocks or fixed frame positions.

| Step | Purpose and required substance |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Recognizable hook | Establish a specific everyday moment and the question or friction that will be resolved. Vary the entry point and viewpoint. |
| 2. Bridge | Connect that experience to the concept through a relevant distinction, possible interpretation, question or action. |
| 3. Name and meaning | Say the established English concept name and its Indonesian meaning early. Naming the concept alone is insufficient. |
| 4. Plain-language explanation | Explain what the concept means so the viewer could restate it. Use accessible Indonesian rather than a formal dictionary passage. This can share a sentence or beat with step 3. |
| 5. Discussion through the scene | Show how the concept relates to the sequence of actions/responses: what happened, what may have been experienced, what was understood or missed, and what the concept helps distinguish. Develop how the process works to the extent supported by research. Return to the opening experience with a clearer interpretation and relevant limits. |
| 6. Concrete application | Derive one usable response, adaptable line, observation or next-step criterion from that explanation. Explain the role of the response instead of stacking unrelated tips. |
| 7. Closing answer | Return to the opening question and leave one specific takeaway. Resolve the question before any optional save/share invitation. |

Make the concept explanation and discussion the center of the episode. As a flexible planning guide, give the plain-language explanation, scene-based discussion and resulting understanding together roughly half the narration. Adjust for the actual concept and delivery; this is not a fixed word, time or frame quota. A name plus a one-sentence definition followed by a long list of tips is insufficient.

The discussion must connect the concept to observable details in the scene, explain the relevant process or distinction, and preserve the limits of the interpretation. Do not manufacture a causal mechanism where the evidence supports only a description or association. A hypothetical inner experience remains a possibility rather than a fact about another person's motive.

For example, the reviewed active-listening sample uses a story cut short by advice, distinguishes what part of the story is difficult, explains checking an interpretation, then shows the speaker correcting that interpretation. The practical question follows from that explanation. The fictional dialogue is illustrative; definition and practice boundaries can be traced through [the existing episode research](../../productions/cerita-belum-selesai/SOURCES.md).

Three treatments remain available within this structure: opening up an everyday scene; making an interaction visible; distinguishing two easily confused things. Vary viewpoint between the person speaking, the person responding and the interaction. Responsibility need not be symmetrical.

Before script review, check that the opening varies meaningfully from recent episodes, the bridge fits its reasoning, the English name and Indonesian meaning are spoken, the concept is explained beyond its label, the scene illustrates that explanation, and the practical response and ending answer the original question. The production workflow still owns research, hook selection and review checkpoints.

## Duration, 16 frames and internal beats

- Exactly **16 meaningful shots/compositions** per standard episode. A subtitle update is not a new frame.
- Retain approximately **90 seconds**, planning range **85–95 seconds**, with **170–200 initial spoken words**. Measure the actual delivery and do not speed narration to force a word count.
- Plan multiple meaningful animation beats within every frame. **3–5 beats** is an initial implementation guideline, adjusted for shot length and reading. A beat changes an action, response, attention, spatial relationship, explanatory emphasis or visible consequence; text entrances and subtitle changes alone do not satisfy the request.
- Speech paragraphs may cross frame boundaries. A frame can develop one idea through several actions. Keep one reading priority at any instant.
- Allocate the sixteen frames after developing the script. Preserve room for concept explanation and discussion without assigning the seven narrative functions identical frame positions in every episode.
- The ending can have several restrained changes followed by a deliberate hold. Richer animation does not require constant loops or rapid text replacement.

Illustrative duration template: **5, 5, 6, 5, 6, 6, 6, 6, 5, 6, 6, 6, 5, 6, 5, 6 seconds = 90 seconds**. Frame positions and final timings follow the researched script and measured narration.

## Two dramatic inversions — user requirement

Exactly **2 of 16 frames** use charcoal background with cream text. Fourteen use cream background with charcoal text. Choose the two dramatic moments from the script: for example an interaction reaching a painful consequence, then a significant realization. Record each frame's dramatic line and the reason for the switch in the storyboard.

Example positions 04 and 12 on the style board demonstrate rhythm only. Other positions are valid. Prefer separate dramatic moments with cream frames between them so each inversion reads as a switch. The script must provide genuine turns; do not introduce invented harm or unsupported psychology to satisfy the color rule.

Switch background, main/supporting text, labels, captions and chrome together. Preserve their geometry. Return to cream for surrounding frames. See frame.md for exact tokens and actor continuity.

## Reading roles

Narration supplies context and nuance. Emphasis typography names a short question, distinction or quoted response when needed. Start with 0–5 emphasis words as a planning guideline; frames can have no emphasis copy. Necessary definitions and quoted responses can be longer if staged readably. This is an accepted implementation guideline for the user's reduction request; the user did not specify a numerical word limit.

Illustrated actions and objects explain the scene: a message space opens, a phone takes attention, a clock remains unresolved, a response returns, or a shared event receives room. Use context-bearing details and large interactions to make the frame legible when emphasis copy is hidden. Labels are added only when the representation would otherwise be ambiguous. Remove supporting paragraphs and duplicate explanatory sentences.

Captions retain all spoken words in their own band. Reducing on-screen emphasis does not remove the question or its nuances from narration/captions. Caption changes do not fulfill the multiple-animation-beat requirement. Richer scenes still develop one clear point at a time.

## Sources and interpretation

Separate observed behavior, possible experience, interpretation and supported explanation. Check the definition, scientific/popular status and limits of a concept before using it. Association does not authorize causal claims. An invented inner monologue does not establish another person's motive. Compressed scripts and animated diagrams retain these distinctions.

Episode research maps claims to sources and records limits; show sources concisely in the caption/description. Use author/year in a frame when it helps. Fictional board studies demonstrate design only and are not ready-to-publish psychology explanations. Production research follows the shortform-production research contract.

## Storyboard handoff

For each of 16 frames, record duration, narration span, main/label copy, focal scale/composition, starting state, timed internal beats, final state, continuity to the next frame, palette mode and sound triggers. Mark exactly two dramatic frames with their script lines. Inspect the full sequence for meaningful composition and motion variation, then inspect actual within-frame changes in production.
