# Shot direction — board v4

Applies to future productions selecting Di Balik Benda v4. This is conceptual production guidance, not an animated demonstration or an episode storyboard. All object views are locked to layered 2.5D illustration.

## Goal

Let viewers discover successive relationships inside a complex mechanism. Maintain a clear spatial model while changing the explanatory task, motion behavior, scale, and pace. Increase visible action and reaction, guide attention between components, and synchronize sound to interactions. Keep the 90-second duration and 14–18-shot planning range; give every shot a new job.

## Design a chain of events

Before authoring animation, map the selected mechanism as **input → interacting assembly → next affected assembly → output → reset or alternate state**, using only supported stages. Identify where motion is transferred, constrained, redirected, stored, released, or gated. Do not require every kind of event in every mechanism.

Choose one visibly trackable landmark and preserve its identity between exterior, exploded, sectional, and macro views. This can be a marked contact, component, path, or assembly axis. An episode may follow several components, but should explicitly hand attention from one to the next.

## Complementary visual treatments

Use a purposeful combination according to the selected mechanism. These are available tools, not a fixed menu that every episode must exhaust.

| Explanatory job | Visual treatment | What must change or become visible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Establish the operating question | Whole object operating under a defined input | A visible result that the breakdown will explain |
| Reveal assembly relationships | Controlled disassembly with retained axes | Which functional parts connect and where they belong |
| Expose a hidden interaction | Local section, transparent housing, or interior angle | Actual contact, clearance, or an internal route |
| Follow a transfer | Persistent assembly with progressive emphasis | A preceding part's state affects the next part |
| Inspect a decisive event | Stable macro, controlled slow observation, or state hold | The exact engagement, deformation, transfer, or release |
| Understand coordinated movement | Multiple physically coupled components operating together | Their synchronization and dependency remain legible |
| Compare operating states | Aligned state comparison or a brief matched replay | A researched change in input, load, direction, or constraint |
| Recover context | Reassembly and whole-system operation | Local interactions explain the visible result |

A camera change earns its place by exposing information. Within the 2.5D lock, pan to track a traveling part, push toward an internal assembly, or pull back to relate two subassemblies. Use restrained layer parallax with coherent illustrated depth. Reveal an occluded contact through a cutaway or a deliberately oriented alternate 2.5D view with shared landmarks. Preserve orientation during the move and hold at decisive contact when that improves reading. Do not introduce true 3D camera orbits or switch to a standalone flat 2D scene.

## Mechanical choreography

- Describe the specific action of each relevant part, its starting state, ending state, and dependency on another part. Examples of motion verbs are rotate, translate, engage, flex, compress, release, advance, and return; use only the verbs that match the verified mechanism.
- Animate the path and the intermediate contact, not just two static poses with a fade. The diagnostic 2–4-pose guidance is a planning minimum for difficult actions, not a limit on animated intermediate states.
- Coupled parts move concurrently when the mechanism requires it. Annotation and framing can guide attention while the physical system remains coherent.
- Keep stationary housings stationary and rigid parts rigid. A section reveal may change visibility, not the actual mechanical behavior.
- Preserve assembly state across shots. A new label or camera framing must not reset the operation to the same starting pose.
- Replays have a new question: slower contact inspection, a newly exposed interior, or a genuinely different operating state. Do not repeat an unchanged loop as the main content of several shots.

## Interaction and attention choreography

Every narrated causal interaction must be visibly animated through input, contact or transfer, and response. Use the following sequence as a planning lens, not four mandatory separate shots:

1. **Orient:** cue the active part with a brief local emphasis, anchored force marker, or a path reveal before the decisive event. Keep the cue attached to its component.
2. **Act:** show the travel, constraint, or engagement through intermediate states. Vary action duration according to mass, travel, and explanatory need.
3. **React:** show the affected component respond with mechanically correct timing. Handoff the emphasis from input orange to inspected blue and output teal where those roles apply.
4. **Confirm:** synchronize the event sound and hold the result long enough to understand. Let attention settle before the next explanatory cue.

Distribute supported interactions throughout the explanation, rather than animating the opening and leaving most later shots as labeled stills. Quiet holds remain useful after observable changes. Review any stretch longer than roughly 5 seconds that has narration about changing mechanism state but no visible action, evolving path, or new state; record a justified observation hold or redesign the shot. This is an agent-selected review trigger, not a user-specified duration limit.

Vary supported translations, rotations, compression, release, engagement, and flow. Combine coupled actions when the mechanism requires them. Add secondary follow-through only when physically supported. Do not shake the whole scene, add a fake control, or exaggerate rigid parts to create activity.

## Interaction SFX cue sheet

Plan sound during storyboarding. Every designed interaction event must have a synchronized sound assignment or a documented reason for intentional silence. Continuous flow or repeated motion may share one evolving cue rather than a separate transient for every small movement.

| Cue-sheet field | Required decision |
| --- | --- |
| Event and time | Shot ID, component IDs, action, onset, duration, and stop or release |
| Visual anchor | Visible contact, engagement, handoff, flow change, or explanatory reveal |
| Cue role | Physical mechanism sound or stylized editorial guidance |
| Sound character | Material, weight, texture, and envelope appropriate to that event |
| Synchronization | Match onset to the visible event within one output frame; explain intentional lead or lag |
| Mix and coverage | Narration overlap, cue priority, shared sustained cue, or reason for silence |
| Asset and review | Production-local asset or asset ID, source or generated provenance, and playback result |

Illustrative mappings only; verify against the selected mechanism:

| Interaction type | Candidate cue | Envelope or caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Press, travel, or sliding contact | Tactile press, short material rub, or travel sound | Body follows the movement; avoid a click before contact |
| Engagement, detent, or release | Material-appropriate tick, click, or snap | Transient at the visible state change, short controlled tail |
| Spring loading and return | Restrained tension or return cue when appropriate | Do not imply every spring audibly rings |
| Fluid or air change | Evolving flow, air, or liquid texture | Start, intensity, and stop follow the visual state |
| Cutaway, exploded reveal, or attention handoff | Soft editorial sweep or tonal tick | Identify as stylized; use below the key physical cues |

Avoid one identical click for all materials and generic whooshes on every transition. A quiet physical event can use a clearly stylized attention cue; do not imply recorded realism. Prioritize the decisive event when coupled sounds overlap. Preserve clear narration, controlled cue tails, and brief quiet around observation holds. Music cannot substitute for interaction SFX.

## Rhythm

Plan short orientation and assembly reveals, longer windows for critical interaction, concise transfers between subassemblies, and a readable whole-system result. Put the longest observation where the mechanism is hardest to understand. Vary durations from the content rather than allocating identical time to every shot.

Transitions preserve orientation or mark a new question. Use a continuous reframe where the same assembly persists, or a clear cut to a genuinely different view when that is easier to follow. No mandatory transition effect, easing quota, or background motion. When named effects are actually authored in HyperFrames, follow its registry and runtime contracts.

## Shot map before production

Record these fields for every planned shot:

| Field | Required decision |
| --- | --- |
| Time and purpose | Time range and the new explanatory question |
| State continuity | Assembly state on entry and exit; landmark carried across the cut |
| Parts and action | Relevant subassembly, physical motion verb, and causal dependency |
| Attention handoff | Active component, pre-event cue, affected component, and result hold |
| Interaction sound | Cue-sheet event IDs, onset or sustained range, physical/editorial role, and silence exceptions |
| Framing | Whole, assembly, section, interior, macro, or comparison; 2.5D layer construction and camera purpose |
| Diagnostic poses | The important approach, contact, transfer, and outcome when relevant |
| Rhythm | Brief orientation, longer observation, active transfer, or decisive result |
| Evidence | Source claim or geometry reference for the hidden relation |

## Review for monotonous repetition

Inspect the shot map, contact sheet, and full mechanism motion. Flag a run of three or more shots that repeats the same framing, operation loop, and visual behavior with only labels changing. Treat this as a review trigger, not a mathematical ban: a justified continuous causal event may deserve sustained attention.

For each flagged run, identify what new physical relationship is learned. Merge redundant shots, expose another necessary relation, change the explanatory view, or select a richer topic if the mechanism has no further content. A color change, text animation, dissolve, or zoom alone does not resolve repeated mechanical behavior.

Watch the mechanism sequence with labels or narration temporarily hidden when feasible. Confirm that successive states, causal handoffs, and coordinated motion can still be distinguished; narration can supply terminology and invisible context. Inspect full-speed playback as well as critical poses. Technical validation, a high shot count, and static screenshots do not by themselves establish interesting choreography. Record any inability to perform motion review honestly.

## Review for attention and interaction sound

- Check every narrated causal interaction against its animated input, transfer, and response. A moving label or camera alone does not satisfy coverage.
- Check the cue sheet against the timeline: all designed interaction events are sounded, share an evolving cue, or have a documented intelligibility or contrast reason for silence.
- Inspect decisive contact poses and listen at normal playback speed. Check sound onset against the visible event within one output frame, except a recorded intentional lead or lag. A static contact sheet cannot verify audio synchronization.
- Watch with sound off to check visual causality, then listen to the full mix on phone speakers to check narration and cue audibility. Check that cues direct attention to the current event instead of suggesting an unrelated event.
- Flag long labeled stills over narrated movement, repeated generic sounds, overloaded transients, cues with no visible anchor, and cuts that reset state. Resolve the cause before export and record any review limitation honestly.
