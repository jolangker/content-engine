## Purpose and audience

Explain how familiar objects and small systems work for curious Indonesian-speaking general audiences. Assume no engineering background. Technical viewers should recognize the mechanism; first-time viewers should be able to follow its input, interaction, and output.

## Scope and pillars

- Everyday mechanisms: closures, pumps, switches, click mechanisms, latches.
- Motion and transmission: gears, levers, springs, bearings, and linkages in a selected real application.
- Flow and control: simple valves, dispensing systems, and pressure-driven devices.
- Useful design details: how one small geometry or material choice changes behavior.

These are topic territories, not verified claims or a required release sequence. Do not inherit relationship psychology or political editorial assumptions from the workspace's other boards.

## Topic selection: interacting mechanisms

Prefer a familiar object whose hidden operation contains several interacting subassemblies, linked stages, and a visually rewarding state change. A useful starting target is **3–5 distinct causal interactions across multiple functional parts**, rather than one local contact repeated throughout 90 seconds. This range describes explanatory richness, not a minimum raw part count or a fabrication quota. A fastener-heavy assembly can still contain only one interesting interaction.

Identify the exact operating question and variant. Scope a complex machine to one coherent cycle or subsystem that can be explained in 90 seconds. Keep the difficulty in the mechanism while making the explanation accessible.

For discovery, compare each candidate on:

- **Causal depth:** the linked interactions and how one changes the next.
- **Visual discovery:** what a cutaway, disassembly, internal view, or alternate operating state will reveal.
- **Motion diversity:** different physically justified actions, such as coupled rotation, translation, engagement, deformation, or flow; identify those actually supported by the mechanism.
- **Evidence:** credible support for hidden geometry, contact, timing, and operation of the selected variant.
- **Production scope:** the geometry, layered 2.5D views, interaction sound needs, and observation time needed to show the difficult interaction clearly.

### Audience appeal and topic decision

Evaluate the audience's reason to watch alongside the existing mechanism criteria. Record all eight criteria for shortlisted candidates; do not pick by mechanical complexity alone. Use concise evidence and a `strong / workable / weak` assessment, not an invented numerical prediction of retention.

| Added criterion | Question | Required decision evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognition and proximity | Will the intended viewer recognize the object, situation, or visible behavior? | Name the familiar encounter; if unfamiliar, show how the opening can establish it immediately. |

A weak central question needs reframing; insufficient evidence needs more research or a different candidate. Do not offset missing proof with high complexity or a dramatic hook. Choose for explanatory interest and evidence. Ease of implementation must not be the main reason to select a one-contact object. ## Reusable content concept

Start from a visible behavior or a precise question, reveal the hidden component that makes it possible, and show the causal chain. The viewer should see at least one relation that ordinary exterior footage hides.

### Episode story contract

Before drafting the script, record the following in the episode's hook/story plan. These decisions apply across all available structures below.

1. **Central mystery:** one question about a visible behavior, plus the opening action that makes it concrete. A general topic label such as "how this object works" needs a more precise question. The question must be accurate, understandable without engineering knowledge, and answerable within the selected subsystem.
2. **First discovery:** the first meaningful causal answer, what the viewer will see, and its planned onset. Aim to show it in approximately the first 8–12 seconds; this is an editable planning target, not a scientific threshold. If orientation needs longer, justify it and keep the opening active and informative. Logo, title, camera movement, or a part name alone is not a discovery.
3. **Question chain:** a short ordered list of linked questions. For each, record the visual answer and why it makes the next question necessary. Reveal useful answers throughout the episode; do not withhold an otherwise simple answer until the last second. Three to five questions can be a starting point, adapted to the mechanism rather than a quota.
4. **Signature interaction:** one evidenced physical interaction that carries the strongest visual discovery. Name its approach, contact or transfer, result, framing, observation needs, and sound role. Allocate time from explanatory difficulty and payoff; surrounding beats should lead into and out of it.
5. **Opening-to-ending proof:** record the opening action/state and the final matched demonstration, with the causal detail the viewer can now understand. The final demonstration must answer the central question.

Illustrative narrative shape, not a verified mechanism or mandatory order: "What starts the movement?" → "How does it reach the next part?" → "What controls the result?" → "Can we now explain the original behavior?" Actual questions must come from the chosen variant and evidence. Avoid unrelated trivia or artificial suspense between causal stages.

### Introduce components when the explanation needs them

Introduce a component at its first causal use: show what it does, then supply its useful name. An illustrative phrasing pattern is "Bagian ini menahan putaran. Namanya …"; verify the function and term for the actual episode. Keep the novice viewer oriented with the whole object and stable landmarks. A brief spatial overview is useful when essential to understanding the next action.

Use an exploded view to answer a spatial or assembly question. Do not front-load a catalogue of all components before the first discovery. A part list, labels, or assembly separation alone does not replace an explanation. Subsequent components enter when their dependency becomes relevant; previously introduced components keep their visual identity.

Available structures:

1. **Follow the action:** input → contact or transfer → output. Good for one compact mechanism.
2. **Find the hidden part:** familiar whole → isolate a component → reveal its role → return to context.
3. **Compare two states:** idle versus operating, open versus closed, or two verified implementations.
4. **Understand a failure:** normal operation → specific failure → visible consequence. Use only when supported by sources and appropriate to the topic.

The visual explanation determines the structure. Default format: a **90-second vertical explanation of one mechanism**, with a richer sequence of explanatory shots. A current explicit episode instruction may override this default; inherited older episode timing does not override it for a new production.

## Duration and breakdown depth

Plan **14–18 distinct explanatory shots or storyboard frames**, with roughly 16 as a starting point. This is a planning range, not a fixed scene quota or an upper limit. Shots may belong to the same continuous scene. An added shot must reveal a component, state, contact, spatial relationship, or causal consequence; changing a title or camera angle alone does not add explanatory depth.

Use the extra time to explain the causal chain, inspect anatomy when needed, and trace the operating cycle. Prioritize the signature interaction selected in the story contract. Include at least two useful detail views when the subject supports them, such as the contact surface and the path through an internal assembly. All object views use the locked 2.5D illustrated treatment in frame.md, including sections and alternate views.

For a selected complex mechanism, distribute this time over a linked chain of interactions. The shot range alone does not demonstrate sufficient depth. Do not fill most of the film with differently labeled views of the same tiny movement.

For each critical action, storyboard its relevant intermediate states: initial state, applied input, approach or contact, transfer or deformation, resulting state, and release or reset when the actual mechanism has one. Show 2–4 diagnostic poses for a complex interaction so the motion between the before and after is designed. These poses may be subframes inside a shot; the 14–18-shot range is separate from diagnostic poses. Do not invent mechanical stages to fill a template.

An illustrative allocation totals 90 seconds and 16 shots. The signature interaction may move within this allocation when the causal order requires it:

| Time | Explanatory job | Starting shot allocation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4 s | Show the recognizable behavior and central mystery | 1 |
| 4–12 s | Show the first causal discovery; introduce its component by function | 2 |
| 12–35 s | Follow linked questions and causal handoffs; reveal parts as needed | 4 |
| 35–62 s | Make the signature interaction readable, with its necessary dependencies | 5 |
| 62–80 s | Connect the remaining consequences and show coordinated operation | 2 |
| 80–90 s | Match the opening action and demonstrate the answer in context | 2 |

This is an editable pacing example, not a mandatory sequence or shot count. Reallocate time where the researched mechanism needs it while retaining the story contract's central question, progressive discoveries, signature interaction, and final proof. Avoid a long introduction or component catalogue that consumes the breakdown budget.

Write and measure narration against the 90-second timeline. If the recording is shorter, expand the supported explanation or add justified observation time with meaningful state changes. Do not automatically shrink the composition to the recording length, pad it with an unchanged loop, or slow the whole film merely to hit duration. Check the final timeline and export against 90 seconds to within one output frame, unless the user explicitly chooses a different duration.

Here, "more frames" means more explanatory shots and intermediate mechanism states. It does not request a higher frame rate. Use the production's selected frame rate; a new production may retain the existing 30 fps convention unless its brief says otherwise.

## Voice and language

Use natural Indonesian, short concrete sentences, and accurate names introduced only when needed. Pair a technical term with a visible function. Prefer “bagian ini menekan...” over abstract claims about engineering genius. Avoid sensational language, unsupported superlatives, unexplained jargon, and filler CTAs.

Illustrative prompts only, not verified scripts:

- “Apa yang terjadi di dalam kepala semprotan saat ditekan?”
- “Bagaimana gigi resleting bisa saling mengait?”
- “Bagian mana yang meneruskan gerakan?”

## Signature interaction and demonstrated ending

Give the selected interaction enough screen area and observation time for its contact or transfer to read at phone size. Preserve the 2.5D language and spatial landmarks. Reduce concurrent labels, new annotations, and camera movement at the decisive instant. Use slower observation only when it reveals the mechanism, mark altered timing, and retain physically coherent dependencies. A short result hold gives the viewer time to see what changed. Sound should clarify this event while keeping speech intelligible; prominence comes from contrast and clarity, not a mandated loudness increase.

The signature interaction can occur early, in the middle, or late. Do not delay it solely for suspense when the causal explanation needs it sooner. Other beats receive the time needed to connect and understand it.

End by revisiting the opening action, question, or matched operating state. Use the same recognizable landmark and input when possible; show the hidden cause in a section, localized emphasis, or purposeful matched replay. The viewer should now be able to explain why the opening result happens. Reassembly or a whole-cycle replay earns its time by making that answer visible. Avoid a generic recap, new unrelated facts, or a CTA that interrupts the proof. A failure-led opening may end with a matched normal/failure comparison when that is the researched answer.

## Visual storytelling rules

- Animate the actual relationship being explained: contact, constraint, transfer, deformation, or flow.
- Preserve the identity and location of the active part through a zoom or cut. Reorient intentionally and provide a recognizable landmark.
- Reveal hidden surfaces using cutaway, transparency, separation, or a change of viewpoint. Pick whichever makes the mechanism clearest.
- Use functional labels and arrows only when they resolve a question. Distinguish movement from force and fluid flow.
- Give each beat one clear explanatory question. Physically coupled components must move together when the real operation requires it; trace attention through their relationship without freezing one part arbitrarily. A part may remain still when its operating state genuinely is still.
- If using a cross-section, mark which shell is removed; if separating parts, make clear that the spacing is explanatory.
- Captions contain spoken words; part annotations may add concise names needed to read the diagram.
- The storyboard must list what each shot newly reveals, its time range, question answered and next causal question, first-use component introduction, relevant parts and contact, 2.5D view construction, camera purpose, attention handoff, intermediate poses, and synchronized SFX events. Mark the first discovery, signature interaction, and opening/ending proof pair. Keep a stable component landmark between whole, exploded, sectional, and macro views.
- During verification, inspect the approach, contact, transfer, result, and reset where applicable. A static before/after pair does not establish that a breakdown animation is sufficiently detailed.
- Follow [SHOT-DIRECTION.md](SHOT-DIRECTION.md) to plan continuous mechanical choreography, purposeful framing changes, varied observation time, and the review for monotonous repetition. More transitions or text entrances do not substitute for a richer operating sequence.

## Interaction, attention, and sound

Design more observable action and reaction across the existing causal chain. Every narrated causal interaction needs a visible initiating action, contact or transfer, and resulting state. Use connected moving parts, progressive path emphasis, and short result holds to keep viewers aware of where to look and what just changed. Maintain physical accuracy; do not invent extra mechanical stages to increase activity. The 3–5-interaction richness guide remains adaptable rather than becoming a sound or motion quota.

For every interaction beat, plan **look here → action → response → confirmation**. Lead with one anchored emphasis, carry attention to the affected component, and synchronize a suitable sound to the event. Vary pace between approach, decisive action, and observation. Short visual questions or a before/after comparison may invite viewers to anticipate the next state when useful; they must fit the existing explanation budget.

SFX coverage is required at designed interaction events, including meaningful actuation, contact, engagement, release, and changes in flow when supported. Distinguish actual mechanical sound from stylized explanatory cues. A sustained event can use one evolving sound, and coupled actions can share a cue when separate transients would mask the narration. Record intentional silence with a reason. Use material and action to choose sounds rather than repeating one click or adding a whoosh to every cut.

The storyboard and final review must include an interaction cue sheet and full-speed audio/visual playback. Check event timing, coverage, cue variety, causal readability, and narration intelligibility on phone speakers. Follow SHOT-DIRECTION.md for fields and acceptance checks. These are production requirements; this static board does not demonstrate their execution.

## Sourcing and accuracy

Before an episode, define the exact mechanism and variant. A generic illustration must not silently stand in for every product using a similar exterior.

Prefer manufacturer service diagrams, documented technical models, patents with relevant embodiment details, educational engineering references, and reputable teardowns. A patent demonstrates a described implementation; it does not establish that every marketed object uses it. A teardown establishes the inspected model, not an entire product category.

Trace each important causal statement, part relationship, and operating state to evidence. Cross-check hidden geometry and motion against at least one credible technical source; use a second independent source for ambiguous or contested behavior. Clearly separate creative inspiration from supporting evidence.

Identify stylization: not to scale, compressed timing, displaced parts, highlighted fluid, removed shell, exaggerated clearance, or conceptual arrows. Verify that simplification preserves the relationship being taught. Use original diagrams or appropriately licensed assets and record provenance.

Do not reuse the current style-board geometry as an engineering reference. All four studies are explicitly conceptual. If the available evidence cannot establish the internal action, narrow the claim, choose a better-documented object, or keep it as a non-published study.

## Boundaries

Explain operation without turning the format into unsourced repair, electrical, pressure-system, vehicle, or medical advice. Select additional domain checks when a real episode needs them; do not add blanket disclaimers to ordinary educational copy.
