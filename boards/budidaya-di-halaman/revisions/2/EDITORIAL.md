# Editorial framework

Editorial revision 2.

## Audience and scope

Put the viewer in the role of the person tending a backyard cultivation project. By the end, the viewer should be able to retell what they tried, what got in the way, and what they changed. Keep the biology and mechanics concrete enough for first-time viewers to follow.

| Pillar | Where to look for a story |
| --- | --- |
| Backyard ponds | A household-size fish, shrimp, or eel pond where a shortfall prompts the keeper to change the setup or routine. |
| Hatchery and stock | An attempt to hatch, rear, separate, or shelter stock where the keeper can see what needs adjusting. |
| Feed and harvest | An attempt to grow live feed, separate a harvest, or collect a return flow where the first setup gives an incomplete result. |
| Related cultivation | A small-yard growing project where a visible environmental constraint prompts a practical change the keeper can test. |

## Select the story before the hook

In each topic candidate's short brief, name the keeper's goal, the first attempt and its visible shortfall, the keeper's response, and the observable result of trying again. Anchor the attempt and retest in one yard-scale site and around one focal organism or output. At discovery, write these as editorial hypotheses. During research, confirm that the proposed shortfall and response make sense under the episode's conditions.

A route through a finished system can give the camera something to follow. If that route supplies the entire premise, reframe it around the keeper's practical goal and a visible difference between two attempts. If research supports only the route, use that subject for a mechanism explainer and pick a different angle for this board. Record the selected chain of events in the episode brief before drafting hook options.

## Story shape

1. **Try something.** Let the cover promise a recognizable result or problem. Start playback immediately with something the keeper builds, changes, or tries. In a “Kalau kamu…” hook, give “kamu” an action in the yard or a practical decision to make.
2. **Show what goes wrong.** Let the audience see the shortfall in the scene before naming its cause. Stage it as an event in the illustrated attempt. Show the undersized result, blocked route, or changed condition while the narration points to the same detail.
3. **Make a decision and test it.** Give “kamu” an active verb for the response. Keep the site and relevant landmarks consistent so viewers can see what changed and what happened next. When the first change leaves a real constraint or creates another one, let that visible consequence prompt the next choice. Favor two linked adjustments when the material supports them. Give a single supported adjustment enough screen time to show its test and result.
4. **Pay off the first goal.** Return to a wider view of the same site and show the result under comparable conditions. Show only what the episode has earned, whether that is a cleared path, material collected in a tray, or a verified harvest. An optional last line can invite a separate backyard experiment.

Use conversational Indonesian with “kamu”, short clauses and concrete nouns. Phrases like “tapi” and “ternyata” belong where the viewer sees a change and has a reason to ask about its cause. Use a mechanism detail at the moment it explains the keeper's decision. Give supporting components screen space and keep spoken detail on the cause, action, and result.

### Review the narration

Read the voiceover by itself before script review. Identify the keeper's first attempt, the line that tells what actually went wrong in the scene, and the action the keeper takes in response. Then check the visual cues for the same before-and-after comparison and a final view that answers the hook. If the only setback is worded as “if this happened” or the fix is introduced as “there is a part,” rewrite the beat around the depicted event and the keeper's action.

## Evidence and practical interpretation

For production, identify the species and life stage, cultivation setup, and conditions behind any practical claim. Check recommended actions, quantities, timing, expected outcomes, and animal-welfare implications against relevant aquaculture or cultivation guidance. Favor source material that matches the system and locality. Explain which conditions change the result. When an episode can support a mechanism more clearly than a recommendation, show the mechanism and narrow the claim.

Show the scale and sequence necessary to understand causality. Mark conceptual, exaggerated, magnified, or time-compressed views in the production notes. Distinguish an illustrative process from a tested result. Verify any promised yield or harvest interval before drafting the cover.

## Worked story example

**Kolam lele saat hujan.** This is an illustrative editorial example. The production researches its own practical setup and claims.

| Beat | What the viewer sees | Sample voiceover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First attempt | A new pond sits beside the path. Rain begins. | “Kamu baru bikin kolam lele di pojok halaman. Terus hujan pertama turun.” |
| Visible shortfall | In a cutaway, the water approaches the rim. | “Tapi hujannya makin deras. Airnya udah hampir sampai bibir kolam.” |
| First repair and consequence | The keeper adds an outlet. The water exits and pools over the path. | “Makanya kamu kasih jalan keluar buat airnya. Tapi coba lihat ujung pipanya. Jalan setapaknya malah jadi kubangan.” |
| Next repair and retest | The keeper extends the route to a receiving basin. Later rain repeats in the same view. | “Jadi kamu sambung pipanya sampai ke bak. Pas hujan lagi, airnya keluar lewat jalur itu.” |
| End wide | The viewer can see the whole route and the keeper walking beside the pond after the rain. | “Pas hujannya reda, kamu bisa lewat di samping kolam lagi.” |

For the nila waste subject, tracking a marked particle can reveal the cause or prove the retest. To qualify as a board episode, first frame a practical goal for the keeper and a visible result that prompts them to change the setup. Compare the same system before and after that action within the researched conditions.

## Visual studies

The style board contains independent examples of a whole site, a close view of a mechanism, and a material separation. Use them for framing, visual emphasis, and phrasing within a story. The worked example above is the reference for how to connect beats in a script.
