# Editorial framework

Editorial revision 3.

## Audience and scope

Put the viewer in the role of the person building a backyard cultivation setup from scratch. Start with an empty site, an unassembled setup, or the materials for the initial build. By the end, the viewer should be able to retell what they built, what got in the way, what they changed, and what they now have. Keep the biology and mechanics concrete enough for first-time viewers to follow.

| Pillar | Where to look for a story |
| --- | --- |
| Backyard ponds | Build a household-size fish, shrimp, or eel setup, bring it into use, then change it in response to an observed shortfall. |
| Hatchery and stock | Build a small hatching, rearing, or shelter setup and show how the keeper adjusts it during its first use. |
| Feed and harvest | Start a live-feed or other cultivation setup and follow it through the first operating result or a harvest step earned by the story. |
| Related cultivation | Start a small-yard growing project where a visible environmental constraint prompts a practical change the keeper can test. |

## Select the story before the hook

In each topic candidate's short brief, name the setup being built, its starting state, what the keeper assembles, what starts the cultivation, at least one visible shortfall and response, and the result the keeper can show at the end. Starting with externally sourced stock, seed, or culture is compatible with this format when the site build and first introduction are shown. Anchor the build and retest in one yard-scale site and around one focal organism or output.

Treat a technique demonstrated on existing stock in an operating setup as a possible later beat. A story that opens with harvesting from an already established maggot bin and adds a ramp covers a harvest improvement. For a from-scratch maggot episode, pitch the start of the rearing setup, its first use, the observed problem, and the collection method as a later improvement. Apply the same test to any maintenance, sorting, or mechanism topic.

At discovery, write the chain of events as an editorial hypothesis. Confirm the actual constraints and responses during research, then record the selected chain in the episode brief before drafting hook options. Choose a fuller build angle if the premise supports only one isolated task or cannot sustain the runtime through meaningful events.

## Story shape

1. **Build the first version.** The cover can preview the result. Begin the story at the starting site or with the unassembled materials and put the first build action on screen immediately. Open with “Kalau kamu…” and an action the keeper takes.
2. **Put the setup to work.** Show enough of the assembly to understand the first version, then show the keeper starting the cultivation and what happens in its first use. Mark elapsed time or a changed condition when it matters.
3. **Show what goes wrong.** Give the viewer an observable shortfall before naming its cause. Show the blocked path, incomplete output, or changed condition while the narration points to the same detail.
4. **Change something and test it.** Keep the site and landmarks consistent so the viewer can compare both attempts. Lead with the keeper's decision. When a real consequence of the first repair motivates another, show that consequence and the next adjustment. Use the remaining story time for actual build, operation, and comparison events when one adjustment is enough.
5. **Show what you built.** Return to the wide view, show the setup doing what the episode set out to do, and hold long enough to see the result. Close the narration with two beats in order. First, congratulate the viewer and name the result shown, using “Selamat, kamu sudah punya…” or a natural equivalent. Then ask a spoken question about what to build next, such as “Habis ini, kamu mau bikin apa lagi di halaman?” Tie the congratulation to the demonstrated result.

Use conversational Indonesian with “kamu”, short clauses and concrete nouns. Phrases like “tapi” and “ternyata” belong where the viewer sees a change and has a reason to ask about its cause. Use a mechanism detail at the moment it explains the keeper's decision. Give supporting components screen space and keep spoken detail on the cause, action, and result.

## Duration and event density

The finished episode must run **at least 90 seconds**, including the spoken sign-off and next-build question. Set this floor in the episode brief, estimate speaking and action time at script review, reconcile it with measured narration during assembly, and verify the final runtime at the requested video endpoint. As a planning starting point, map roughly seven to nine meaningful events across the build, first use, setback, response, retest, and result. The event count is flexible when the causal chain earns the runtime. Extend a short cut by developing researched actions and consequences that can be seen. Keep the delivery conversational and give the final image a natural hold.

### Review the narration

Read the voiceover by itself before script review. Identify what the keeper builds, when it goes into use, what actually goes wrong, which action addresses it, and what the retest shows. Check the visual cues for an identifiable starting state, the same before-and-after comparison, and a final view that answers the hook. Check that the estimated cut covers the 90-second floor with meaningful action and ends with both spoken lines. If a setback exists only as a hypothetical, or the fix appears as a part already in place, rewrite the beat around the depicted event and the keeper's action.

## Evidence and practical interpretation

For production, identify the species and life stage, cultivation setup, and conditions behind any practical claim. Check recommended actions, quantities, timing, expected outcomes, and animal-welfare implications against relevant aquaculture or cultivation guidance. Favor source material that matches the system and locality. Explain which conditions change the result. When an episode can support a mechanism more clearly than a recommendation, show the mechanism and narrow the claim.

Show the scale and sequence necessary to understand causality. Mark conceptual, exaggerated, magnified, or time-compressed views in the production notes. Distinguish an illustrative process from a tested result. Verify any promised yield or harvest interval before drafting the cover.

## Worked story example

**Kolam lele saat hujan.** This is an illustrative editorial example. The production researches its own practical setup and claims.

| Estimated cut | Event | What the viewer sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 00:00–00:10 | Choose the spot | The keeper marks where a pond will sit beside the path in an empty yard. |
| 00:10–00:24 | Assemble the first version | The keeper raises the frame, lays the liner, and adds water. Keep the rim visible for the later comparison. |
| 00:24–00:35 | Put it into use | After an indicated preparation period, the keeper introduces the stock. The wide view establishes the path and the normal waterline. |
| 00:35–00:47 | First rain exposes a shortfall | A cutaway shows the surface approaching the rim. |
| 00:47–01:01 | First repair | The keeper adds an outlet. Track the first water leaving the pond. |
| 01:01–01:13 | The exit creates another problem | The water collects by the path. The keeper stops in front of the puddle. |
| 01:13–01:27 | Reroute and retest | The keeper extends the route to a receiving basin. When rain returns, follow the water all the way to its destination. |
| 01:27–01:37 | Result and spoken ending | Return to the wide view and show the keeper walking past the pond. “Selamat, kamu sudah punya kolam lele dengan jalur limpasan. Habis ini, kamu mau bikin apa lagi di halaman?” |

For the nila waste subject, a marked particle can reveal the cause or prove the retest within a story about the keeper building and putting a backyard setup into use. It gives the comparison a visible focal point.
